# Constitutive and Customary

*The operation underneath the whole portfolio — what it is, what it can change, how far it reaches, and how to say it to someone outside the field.*

*Companion to `requirement-validity.md` (the naming and the seven knowledge resources) and `essential-requirements.html` (the four tracks of work available now). Where those documents cover what to build, this one covers why it matters, how far it goes, and what holds it together.*

*Status: worked out in conversation. Proposals and formulations, not decisions taken. The framework claim in Part I is untested and should be treated as a hypothesis until the corpus demonstrates it on one object.*

---

## Part I — The framework

### The one operation

**Separate what is constitutive from what is merely customary. Then find another route to the constitutive thing.**

Everything in the portfolio is this operation with a different object in front of it. It is not a plan and not a metaphor; it is a procedure that produces different answers depending on what it is pointed at.

### Five fields, one operation, no cross-citation

| Field | Its name for the same operation |
|---|---|
| Educational measurement | Construct vs. construct-irrelevant variance |
| Human rights law | *Bona fide* requirement vs. inherited practice |
| Disability studies | The capability vs. the normate body assumed to perform it |
| Implementation science | Core components vs. the adaptable periphery |
| Cultural adaptation | Surface vs. deep structure |

Each field discovered it independently. None of them cite one another.

### The claim underneath

**Fidelity and validity are the same question.**

Both ask: *what must be held constant for the inference — or the outcome — to survive, and what may vary freely?*

Assessment specialists have worked on this for seventy years under one name; implementation scientists for thirty under another. They do not read each other. The position that makes this visible is the intersection where both objects appear at once — standards that gate entry to a profession, and interventions that must survive being handed to someone else.

The scarcity described in `requirement-validity.md` is a local instance of a larger one. Four literatures do not sit in one person for requirement validity. Five do not sit in one person for this.

### The four moves, as a procedure

1. **Name the construct.** What must actually happen for the outcome to occur?
2. **Find the method wearing the construct's clothes.** What has been treated as the thing when it is one route to the thing?
3. **Ask whose route it is.** Which body, which credential, which culture does the customary method assume — and who was absent when it was chosen?
4. **Build and evidence another route**, then record the reasoning so someone can revise it later.

**Move three is load-bearing and easiest to drop.** Without it this is decomposition, which management consultants have sold for fifty years. Move three is what makes it this work rather than generic quality improvement.

### How the object grows, by phase

| Phase | Object | What the operation produces | Authority required |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ground** | Documents and admissions standards | The corpus, the reframing bank, the defensibility schema, first papers | None |
| **Frame** | Assessments and programme design | The review protocol, tested rather than proposed. Moves from what a standard *says* to what an examination actually *rewards* — the OSCE station scoring auscultation technique while claiming to measure respiratory assessment | Leeds, LSHTM |
| **The Room** | Your own practice | Manualising Make Space *is* this operation, turned inward: which parts of what happens in that room are constitutive of the outcome, and which are your particular facilitation, voice, materials, taste | Registration |
| **The Question** | The workforce | *Which components of this intervention require a registered clinician, and which require only training?* The task-shifting question, stated exactly | Doctoral resources |
| **Deployment** | The framework itself | Published as method, taught as course, argued in a book | Standing |

**The transfer that matters.** Everyone who manualises anything discovers they cannot tell habit from necessity, and that most of what felt essential was habit. By the time that phase arrives you will have spent years performing exactly this discrimination on other people's standards. That is why the standards work is not a detour from the clinical work — it is the training for the hardest thing the clinical work requires.

**The symmetry that unifies the career.** Entry to the profession and delivery of the care are the two ends of one pipeline. *Which requirements are genuinely essential* and *which components require a clinician* are the same sentence with the direction reversed. This framework is what makes them one problem instead of two careers.

### Why it qualifies as a framework

- **It is scale-invariant.** The same four moves apply to a paragraph, an exam, an intervention, a workforce, and a field.
- **It requires no re-learning at each stage.** What each credential buys is not a new method but permission to point the existing one at a larger object. That property is what distinguishes a framework from a sequence of projects.
- **It survives the delete-a-move test.** Without move one you accommodate without knowing whether you have broken the measurement. Without move three you rebuild the same default body with better documentation.

### Cautions

**Universal frameworks are cheap.** Almost every one is true and useless — it reframes without changing any output. Keep applying the delete-a-move test. The failure mode of a framework this portable is that it starts explaining everything and predicting nothing.

**Do not claim the whole thing publicly before the corpus exists.** *Validity and fidelity are the same question* is either a real contribution or an overreach, and the difference is entirely whether it can be demonstrated on one object first. Do the standards work, publish it, and let the larger claim arrive as a discovery rather than as an announced thesis.

## Part II — Impact

### The leverage argument

**A technical standards document is a page of text.** Changing it costs nothing material — no retrofit, no staffing, no budget line, no capital request. That is unusual in accessibility work, where almost every improvement has a cost attached and therefore a hardship argument available against it.

Here the cost of change is near zero and the cost of *not* changing is borne entirely by people outside the institution. That is exactly the configuration in which things stay unchanged for decades, because nobody inside feels the friction.

**And a single document governs a whole cohort, every year, indefinitely.** Individual accommodation work is one student at a time and resets each September. Standards work is one edit multiplied by every applicant who reads it. The ratio of effort to reach is better here than almost anywhere else in the access field.

### The population that matters most is uncounted

The people affected are mostly *not* the students who reach an accommodation office and lose an argument. They are the ones who read the document at application stage, recognise themselves as excluded, and never apply.

- No institution counts them.
- No tribunal ever hears from them.
- They do not appear in the accommodation statistics programmes cite when they say the system works.

This is why the intervention point sits upstream of every accommodation service in the country, and why **code 3** — the self-assessment request that converts the document into a disclosure event — does more work than its position in the coding frame suggests. If the corpus shows most programmes ask applicants to certify themselves against method-level requirements before applying, that finding alone describes a filter operating on a population nobody has measured.

### The long-run effect: who the professions are made of

A profession that has quietly selected for a particular body for a century has a corresponding blind spot in the care it delivers. **The disabled practitioner excluded at admission is also the clinician some future patient does not get.**

If the mission is accessible care for depression, standards work is one of the few levers acting on the *composition* of the workforce rather than on its training — and composition is upstream of a great deal that training then has to compensate for. This is the connection that makes the standards work part of the portfolio rather than beside it.

### Where the leverage actually sits

**Not the sixty programmes.** Programmes change their documents when an accreditor asks them to. The bodies that could make this change across an entire profession at once are few — CASN, AFMC and CAME, provincial regulators, the counselling and social work accreditors. Persuading one of them to put construct-level phrasing into a standard does more than fifty workshops.

**The other short route is legal.** One tribunal decision adopting the construct-versus-method distinction as the test for whether a requirement is *bona fide* would reshape institutional behaviour faster than any amount of publication, because legal risk moves faculties that argument does not. Papers cited in human rights adjudication have outsized effect relative to their citation counts.

This makes the framework piece potentially the highest-impact single artifact — not because many people will read it, but because of who might.

**Both routes argue for the same discipline:** write for small audiences with disproportionate reach, and make the artifacts citable and quotable rather than merely persuasive.

### The honest limits

**Document change is not practice change.** This is the evidence-to-practice gap, in your own area of expertise. A rewritten standard sitting above an unchanged placement culture, unchanged preceptors, and unchanged informal beliefs about who can do the work produces a better document and roughly the same outcomes. Stage 4 of the review protocol is a partial answer only.

**The field is small.** JPED's readership is modest; CJDS's is smaller. Most academic papers do nothing to anything. The realistic near-term picture is a dataset, two or three papers, a handful of consulting engagements, and slow citation accrual. **The most certain impact in the first two years is on your own position rather than on the world.** That is not a reason not to do it — but the essential requirements document is partly a career document, and it is worth keeping clear which impact is nearly certain and which is speculative.

### The serious hazard: the instrument running backwards

**A defensibility framework can be used to defend.** A programme that runs a rigorous-looking review, documents its reasoning carefully, and arrives at the same exclusion is now *harder* to challenge than when its standard was an undefended paragraph. You would have supplied the paper trail.

This happens routinely with equity audits and impact assessments: the process improves, the outcome does not, and the process becomes the evidence that the outcome is fine.

**Two guards, neither of which is a disclaimer in an introduction:**

1. The protocol must **require** disabled practitioners as reviewers, not recommend it.
2. The applicant-facing guide must exist, so the framework is available to both sides of any dispute.

An asymmetrically distributed instrument becomes an institutional weapon by default, not by intent.

### What would count as evidence it worked

Written down now, before there is a stake in a flattering answer:

- A programme's revised document uses the construct/method distinction **without citing you** — the sign it has become how people think rather than something they read.
- **Another programme copies the revised document.** These documents are notoriously borrowed between institutions, which is how the bad phrasing propagated; the same mechanism runs in reverse. This means the reframing bank's real goal is to become the thing people copy.
- A tribunal, court, or human rights commission cites the framework.
- An accreditation standard changes its language.
- Someone you have never met publishes in the vocabulary.
- **A disabled applicant says the guide changed whether they applied.**

The last is the smallest and the only one that is unambiguously the thing itself.

## Part III — Above the documents

Document work is the entry point because text is the only part of this system a person with no affiliation, budget, or institutional role can touch on a Tuesday. That is a property of the entry point, not the ceiling. Four layers sit above it.

### 1. Assessment redesign — where standards actually bite

Rewriting a requirement to state a construct achieves nothing if the assessment still recognises only one performance. If the standard says *assess respiratory status* but the OSCE station awards marks for auscultation technique, the document has changed and the gate has not.

The substantive work is redesigning assessment so it can evidence the same construct through more than one route, at the same standard, defensibly scored: building instruments, writing rubrics, training examiners, defending the result to an accreditor. **This is where fifteen years of learning-systems work is decisive, and it is the piece the measurement people, the lawyers, and the critics all cannot do.**

### 2. Clinical educator training — where the decisions are actually made

Most exclusions do not happen at admission. They happen in week four of a rotation, when a preceptor with no training in any of this decides in good faith that a student "isn't safe" — with no record, no criterion, and no appeal, because it was framed as professional judgement rather than a decision about a standard.

Nothing in a document reaches that person. What reaches them is training for clinical educators: how to separate capability from method under time pressure, what to document, when to ask for help. A curriculum, delivered to people. The least document-like work on the list.

### 3. Individual cases

Once the framework exists, being the person who writes the analysis that keeps a specific student in a specific programme. Slow, unscalable, one at a time — and the reason the rest is worth doing. The corpus does not replace this work; it makes it winnable more often.

### 4. Accreditation and law

An accreditation standard rewritten, or a tribunal adopting the distinction as the test, moves every programme at once without editing any of their documents. Influence on rules rather than on text.

### The hierarchy

| Layer | Cost / access | Leverage |
|---|---|---|
| Documents | Cheapest, available immediately | Real but shallow |
| Assessment redesign | Requires invitation and expertise | Where standards bite |
| Educator training | Requires curriculum and reach | Where daily decisions happen |
| Accreditation and law | Hardest access | Highest |

**Start at the top because it is the only unlocked door. Do not stay there.**

## Part IV — The scope of the work

### The one question underneath everything

**How do you take care that works in one room, with one person, and make it deliverable — faithfully, by someone who isn't you, at scale, to people the system currently doesn't reach?**

Depression is the domain. Access is the constraint that most people treat as an afterthought and this work treats as the design condition. The scope only looks sprawling if this question isn't visible.

### Six kinds of work, genuinely different in kind

**The room.** Direct therapeutic practice with people experiencing depression. Smallest in reach, largest in what it authorises — you cannot credibly say how an intervention should be trained out if you have never held the thing yourself. It is also the permanent correction to systems thinking.

**The program.** Make Space and Other Bodies becoming something that travels: outcome measurement, manualisation, facilitator curriculum, train-the-trainer, cultural adaptation with local leads holding first authorship. The endpoint is an intervention running in rooms you are not in.

**The method.** The rarest thing held and currently the least named: how you take something that worked once and build the training system that lets a non-specialist deliver it without degradation. A methodology, not a service, and publishable as one. Also the income that funds the credential years, which is not a lesser role.

**The gate.** Requirement validity and standards defensibility — who is permitted to become a practitioner. Inside the same question rather than beside it: task-shifting asks who can be trained to deliver care; essential requirements decide who is allowed to try. Same question, opposite end of the pipeline.

**The evidence.** Reviews, qualitative studies of participant experience, feasibility work, implementation studies, the corpus study. What converts a practice you believe in into a claim others can act on.

**The record.** Fieldwork, the studio journal, papers, conference work, teaching, keynotes, the book. The mechanism by which the method reaches people who will never hire you.

**And the meaning layer** — Dear Ordinary, the fine art practice. Constitutive rather than indulgent, and it belongs in the scope explicitly rather than smuggled in as recovery time.

### What each credential actually unlocks

| Stage | What it buys |
|---|---|
| **Now, with none** | More than the plan credits: the corpus, publishing, consulting, facilitation, conference abstracts. Nobody checks credentials before accepting an abstract or reading a dataset. |
| **CPACC + health-sector role** | Institutional legibility — the marker that lets an organisation hire you into work you can already do |
| **Leeds** | Formalises the critique lens; gives the standards work its home discipline |
| **LSHTM** | Epidemiology and task-shifting literature — permission to speak about scale without hand-waving |
| **Counselling MA + registration** | The room, and the standing to say *this is what happens clinically* rather than *this is what the literature says* |
| **Doctorate (optional)** | Three things and not much else: grant eligibility, the right to supervise others' research, access to questions needing resources you cannot assemble alone |

**The scope does not expand evenly with credentials. Most of it is available now.** What credentials add is *authority over claims*, not permission to work.

### The resolution: task-shifting applied to yourself

The scope as described is larger than one working life. If the plan is that you personally execute all of it, the plan is wrong — and the failure mode won't be dramatic. It will be everything running at forty per cent and nothing reaching the standard that makes it matter.

The resolution is already inside the thesis: **the endpoint of this work is that most of it is done by people you trained.** Facilitators running Make Space in rooms you have never entered. Accommodation advisors reasoning across four literatures because they took a course, not because they happen to be you. Local leads publishing adaptation studies under their own names.

**The scope is only impossible if you are the one holding it.** Task-shifting is not just the subject of the work — it is the operating model of the career, applied to yourself.

*Practical consequence:* whenever you can build the training system instead of doing the thing, that is the higher-scope choice, even when it feels like a step away from the work.

### The two constraints

**Some parts compete rather than compound.** Clinical hours and research hours come from the same day. A consulting contract accepted is research time spent. The discipline is naming those trades out loud rather than letting them happen invisibly. The standards work is unusually good on this axis, because it runs at a desk during the years when everything clinical is gated behind credentials still being acquired.

**A scope this wide can be a genuine synthesis or a way of never choosing, and from the inside those feel identical.** The test is whether the parts feed each other in ways that can be named specifically. Mostly here they do — the studio generates the data, the consulting generates the case studies, the standards work generates the access framework, the room keeps all three honest. **If a part has run for three years and no other part has drawn on it, that is a signal to take seriously rather than explain.**

### The single artifact that proves the scope was real

A creative-health intervention for depression, made transferable by a training system you designed, accessible by construction rather than by accommodation, delivered by people who are not you, in a setting that would never have had access to it, with evidence attached.

Everything in the scope is either a component of that artifact or a capability required to build it. **Anything that turns out to be neither is optional, however interesting.**

## Part V — Explaining it outside the field

### The one sentence

> Before you can train as a nurse or a doctor, the school publishes a list of things you must be able to do. A lot of those lists describe one particular way of doing the job rather than the job itself — and written that way, they screen people out for no good reason.

### The example that does the work

> A nursing programme might say you must be able to hear a blood pressure through a stethoscope. But the actual job is measuring blood pressure accurately. A deaf nurse with an amplified or digital stethoscope does that job perfectly well. The requirement, as written, excludes her — not because she can't do the work, but because of how someone described the work.

Most people get it here, and immediately supply a second example from their own field.

### The thirty-second version

> These documents get copied between institutions and almost none say when they were last reviewed or by whom. So they're mostly inherited — someone wrote down how the job was done in 1985, and it's been passed along since. Nobody has ever had to defend them, because the people they exclude usually just don't apply. They read the list, recognise themselves as ineligible, and that's the end of it. Nobody counts those people. I'm collecting the documents and going through them systematically, which apparently nobody in Canada has done.

*"Nobody has done this" is the line a lay listener finds genuinely surprising, and the one that produces a follow-up question.*

### The scarcity, in lay terms

> Because fixing one takes four kinds of expert who never sit in the same room — someone who knows how to test whether a measurement actually measures what it claims, someone who knows discrimination law, someone who thinks critically about disability, and someone who knows how professional training programmes are actually built. Every university has all four. No single job requires more than two of them.

People and jobs, which anyone can picture — not faculties and citation networks, which nobody outside a university can.

### The objection you will always get

> *"But some of those requirements are real — I don't want a surgeon who can't see."*

Agree immediately, then turn it:

> Exactly — and this makes the real ones stronger. Right now a genuine safety requirement and an inherited habit are written in the same voice, with the same authority, and neither has evidence attached. That's bad for everyone. If you can show which ones are load-bearing, you can defend them properly. The ones that fall over were never doing anything.

**Never let the first thirty seconds sound like *lower the standards*.** It sounds like that by default if you lead with exclusion rather than with accuracy — and once it's filed under "lowering standards," you don't get it back in that conversation.

### What not to say first

- **Not** *construct validity*, *requirement validity*, or *competence standards*. Field vocabulary is for the field. Say "what the job actually requires versus one way of doing it."
- **Not** the four literatures as the opening. Naming your inputs makes it sound like an academic exercise; naming the problem makes it sound like a problem.
- **Not** *ableism* or *the normate body* to a general audience — true, load-bearing in writing, and it converts a listener who was nodding into one deciding whether they're being accused of something.
- **Not** the career framing. *"I'm building a research position in this"* is honest but makes the listener evaluate you rather than the problem. It comes second, if they ask.

### The closer

> Every one of these is a page of text. Changing it costs nothing — no money, no building, no staff. It's just that nobody has ever been asked to.

## Open questions carried forward

- Can *validity and fidelity are the same question* be demonstrated on a single object, or does it stay a framing device? The corpus is the test.
- Which of the four layers above documents is realistically reachable in the Ground and Frame phases, and which genuinely wait for standing?
- Is there a route to an accreditor or regulator that does not require the credential years to have completed first?
- What is the mechanism that stops the defensibility instrument from being used to defend — beyond the two guards named, which are necessary but may not be sufficient?
- How does the meaning layer get protected when the portfolio is under load, given that it is the first thing that looks optional and the thing the vision documents argue is constitutive?
