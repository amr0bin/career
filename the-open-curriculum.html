Training · Free and low-cost

The open curriculum

Everything reputable that can be studied without enrolment, permission, or tuition — sequenced against the CPACC, the Leeds MSc, and the long road to counselling psychology. Cost and availability are stated plainly, including where something is not actually free.

How to read this. Nothing here substitutes for the credentials already in the plan. What free training does is different: it closes specific knowledge gaps ahead of paid study, it produces certificates that make a CV legible to hiring managers who cannot assess fifteen years of learning design at a glance, and — in two cases below — it puts a person in a supervised room with people in distress, which no course can do. Verify enrolment windows before planning around any single item; several of these run on annual or seasonal intakes.
Track one

Rights, disability, and the international frame

This is CPACC Domain I territory and Leeds preparation at the same time. It is also the track with the strongest free offerings anywhere — the UN and WHO have invested heavily here, and the certificates carry real institutional weight.

WHO QualityRights e-training on mental health, recovery and community inclusion
World Health Organization

The single best fit in this entire list. It sits precisely on the intersection the vision is built around — psychosocial disability, human rights, and mental health services — and it argues the position from inside the WHO rather than from the outside. Covers stigma, coercion, supported decision-making, and the transformation of services toward a person-centred, rights-based recovery approach, all framed against the CRPD.

Free, open to anyone, WHO certificate on completion. Available in eleven languages.

FreeCertificateCPACC Domain ILeeds prep
UN Disability Inclusion Strategy — Putting Words into Action
UN System Staff College, with the Executive Office of the Secretary-General

Ninety minutes, self-paced, scenario-based. Written for UN personnel at all levels but useful well outside that: it is a working model of how a large institution operationalises disability inclusion across every function rather than confining it to an accommodations office. That is exactly the organisational-change argument an Accessibility Advisor has to make. The module was itself designed with accessibility in mind, which makes it worth studying twice — once for content, once for craft.

Inclusive Communication Module
UNICEF, via Agora and UN SDG:Learn

Three parts: CRPD background and recommended disability terminology; meaningful participation of disabled people in communications work; practical accessibility in communications and events. Aimed primarily at UNICEF staff but explicitly open to partners and the general public. Short, and directly transferable to learning-design deliverables.

Track two

Accessibility standards and the built environment

CPACC Domains II and III. The digital side is already a fifteen-year strength; the honest gaps are the built environment, procurement, and the legal frameworks. That is where these should be pointed.

Digital Accessibility Foundations
W3C Web Accessibility Initiative with UNESCO IITE, on edX

The authoritative introduction, built on the W3C WAI open curricula and taught by trainers from Deque, Intopia, Knowbility, TPG, Infoaxia, and Web Key IT. Covers what web accessibility is, people and digital technology, the business case, and first steps in applying WCAG and WAI-ARIA.

Free to audit, with full access to the content; an optional verified certificate is US$99. Scheduled to remain on edX through at least November 2026. Given the CPACC is already funded, the $99 is the cheapest credible certificate on this page — worth taking for the CV line alone.

Free to audit$99 certificateCPACC Domains II–III
AccessibleEU online campus
AccessibleEU Resource Centre, European Commission

Free, no-cost, self-paced MOOCs with no live sessions, open to anyone. The recurring catalogue has included Introduction to Accessibility of the Built Environment (ten modules, roughly 35 hours, covering universal design, user diversity, legal and policy frameworks, standards, procurement, and accessible housing), An Introduction to Accessible Public Transport (nine modules, roughly 25 hours), and Accessible Technology Design (six modules, roughly 25 hours).

The built-environment course in particular fills the biggest genuine gap between a learning designer's knowledge and the CPACC body of knowledge. The catch: these run on an annual cycle, typically opening around October with registration closing in early December. Framed to European law, so read the legal modules comparatively rather than as Canadian content.

RHFAC Fundamentals Training
Rick Hansen Foundation — a BC organisation

Four weeks, roughly twenty hours, self-paced, with facilitated peer sessions and a certificate and badge. Universal design and meaningful access in the built environment, plus the RHFAC rating methodology. The Professional designation is a separate, longer, more expensive course.

Paid, and the tuition grants currently on offer are for Ontario residents. Listed here because it is the most locally credible built-environment credential in BC and because a future employer may fund it — not as something to buy now.

Track three

Psychological first aid and the humanitarian model

This is the Red Cross track, and it is the closest available approximation of the training stack that makes those practitioners so credible. It is also the honest, self-limiting answer to the counselling-psychology question: PFA is not therapy, and every one of these courses says so. What it builds is the capacity to be useful in a room where someone is in distress, which is the substrate the clinical training will later sit on.

IFRC Psychological First Aid e-learning
International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies, with the Danish Red Cross

Ninety minutes, self-paced. How to comfort someone in distress and help them feel safe and calm; common patterns of reaction to crisis; connecting people to further support; and self-care. Developed under the international Psychological First Aid for All pledge, which means it is the version the Red Cross network itself uses.

Hosted on the IFRC Learning Platform. Registration and use of that platform are completely open and free to the general public, not just Red Cross volunteers, and courses can be downloaded for offline study.

Free90 minThe Red Cross model
IFRC Learning Platform and Health & Care Training Platform
IFRC

Hundreds of short, free, multilingual courses with communities of practice attached, plus a dedicated health hub covering migration and health, community-based health and first aid, climate and health, and MHPSS. Free and open to anyone, not only to network volunteers.

Treat this as the browse-and-build source. Three or four completed courses here plus the PFA module is a coherent humanitarian-health block on a CV, and it is the closest a person can get to the Red Cross training culture without joining a National Society.

Introducing Mental Health and Psychosocial Support (MHPSS) in Emergencies
World Health Organization, on OpenWHO

Around seven hours, taken all at once or across sittings, with a test-based certificate and a shareable digital badge. It covers integrating mental health into general health care in humanitarian settings, the mhGAP Humanitarian Intervention Guide, and — the part that matters most here — scalable psychological interventions: how they are designed, where they fit in a stepped-care model, and what changes when you adapt them for different delivery modes.

That last section is the task-shifting evidence base stated in WHO's own words. It is the direct precursor to the LSHTM Global Mental Health course and, further out, to the conceptual paper about training systems as interventions.

Psychological First Aid — the RAPID model
Johns Hopkins University, on Coursera

Five weeks. Reflective listening, assessment of needs, prioritisation, intervention, disposition, and a final week on self-care. Distinguishing non-incapacitating crisis reactions from more severe ones, and recognising when to facilitate access to further mental health support, is a genuinely clinical skill taught here in a non-clinical form.

Free to enrol and audit. The course states plainly that it does not prepare anyone to render PFA in a disaster setting on its own — which is the correct relationship between it and the supervised practice in Track four.

Track four

Supervised practice — the part no course can replace

This is the track that matters most and the only one with a real deadline. Counselling psychology programs, and the people who write reference letters for them, do not weigh certificates the way they weigh documented hours in a supervised helping role. Everything above is preparation for this.

Crisis Services Volunteer
Crisis Intervention and Suicide Prevention Centre of BC — Vancouver

110 hours of basic training including two to four hours a week of online modules, then 32 hours of supervised call-taking, then a commitment of 250 credited hours of direct service. The Centre describes its training and ongoing supervision as well recognised by universities and helping agencies across the province — which is the whole point of doing it.

Applications for the next group are not currently open. There is a notification form for the November 2026 training group; filling it in does not hold a place. Training carries a fee on a sliding scale, discussed at interview. This is the one item on this page with a clock attached.

Crisis Line Volunteer — remote
Crisis Centre for Northern BC

Roughly seventy hours over six to eight weeks, delivered remotely, with ongoing intakes rather than fixed cohorts. The training program is completely free and includes ASIST certification — the Applied Suicide Intervention Skills Training that costs several hundred dollars to take independently. On-shift supervision is provided to remote responders, and the commitment is one four-hour shift a week for a minimum of one year.

Candidates are accepted across the Northern BC region, so confirm eligibility from the Lower Mainland before planning on it. If it is open, it is the strongest free offer on this page: a recognised suicide-intervention certification and a year of supervised practice at no cost.

safeTALK, ASIST, and Mental Health First Aid
LivingWorks and the Mental Health Commission of Canada, delivered locally

These are the three names that appear on the CVs of nearly everyone in BC's helping professions. safeTALK is a half-day in-person suicide-alertness training; ASIST is the two-day intervention workshop; MHFA covers responding to a mental health or substance use crisis until professional help arrives.

All three are paid when taken independently, but they come bundled into crisis-line volunteer training — sometimes free, sometimes discounted. That bundling is the reason to route through a crisis centre rather than buying them separately.

Track five

Research literacy and ethics

Small track, high leverage. The qualitative paper from Make Space needs an ethics footing, and the Leeds application is stronger for arriving with one.

TCPS 2: CORE — Course on Research Ethics
Interagency Advisory Panel on Research Ethics — CIHR, NSERC, and SSHRC

Nine modules, about four hours, self-paced, free, with a certificate of completion. It is the Canadian standard: every graduate student conducting human-participant research submits this certificate with their ethics application, and most Canadian institutions require it.

Do it before the Make Space paper rather than after. It costs an afternoon and it changes how the consent, privacy, and participant-welfare sections of that paper get written — which is precisely where a paper submitted without a doctoral affiliation is most likely to be challenged.

The gap

What the advisor postings actually ask for

Worth stating directly, because it changes what to study. UBC's Accessibility Advisor postings ask for an undergraduate degree, five to six years of related experience or an equivalent combination, and — the operative line — considerable training and experience in disability, universal design, and duty to accommodate in a post-secondary setting. A graduate degree in a relevant discipline is preferred. The work is described as developing organisational change initiatives, policies, and educational programs, determining and facilitating accommodations, managing complex accommodation cases, and consulting with faculty on how policy affects access.

Read against that, the accessibility knowledge is not the bottleneck. Fifteen years of learning design plus the CPACC covers universal design and standards comfortably. Two things are missing, and neither is a course problem in the way it first appears.

Duty to accommodate as a legal practice. Undue hardship, the interactive process, medical documentation, and the BC Human Rights Code as it applies to educational services. This is human-rights law applied to individual cases, and it is what an Accessibility Advisor does most days. Provincial human rights commission policy guidance and tribunal decisions are free and are the actual source material; the Ontario Human Rights Commission's policy on disability and the duty to accommodate is the most cited plain-language document in the country, useful comparatively even though BC has its own Code. Free webinars from the Government of Canada accessibility hub and the CSPS video series on disability inclusion cover the federal framing.

The post-secondary setting itself. This one is closed by proximity, not study. CACUSS — the Canadian Association of College and University Student Services — runs the accessibility community of practice where these advisors actually gather, and NEADS is the national student-led disability organisation. Membership, conference attendance, and one or two informational conversations with a current UBC or SFU advisor will do more for a candidacy than another certificate.

Sequence

What to actually do, in order

The list above is a menu. This is the meal. Everything here fits alongside a full-time role and the CPACC study design already written.

This week
Join the Crisis Centre BC notification list for the November 2026 group. It takes two minutes, it does not commit to anything, and it is the only item with a closing window.
This week
Email the Crisis Centre for Northern BC to ask whether Lower Mainland residents are eligible for remote training. Free ASIST certification and a year of supervised practice is worth one email to find out.
Month one
WHO QualityRights e-training, then the UNSSC disability inclusion module. Two certificates, strong CPACC Domain I overlap, and the theoretical frame Leeds will assume.
Month two
IFRC Psychological First Aid, then the Johns Hopkins RAPID course. Ninety minutes and then five weeks. The first is the humanitarian standard; the second is the depth behind it.
Month two
TCPS 2: CORE. One afternoon. Do it before drafting the Make Space paper, not after.
Month three
OpenWHO MHPSS in Emergencies. Seven hours, certificate and badge, and the scalable-interventions module that the whole task-shifting argument rests on.
Alongside CPACC
W3C Digital Accessibility Foundations, audited; buy the $99 certificate only if the CPACC slips. Overlaps heavily with CPACC study, so it costs little extra time.
By October
Register for the AccessibleEU built-environment course when the autumn intake opens. Thirty-five hours, free, and the largest genuine gap between a learning designer's knowledge and the CPACC body of knowledge.
Ongoing
Read BC Human Rights Tribunal decisions on educational accommodation, one a month. Free, and closer to the daily work of an Accessibility Advisor than any course on this page.
Availability, fees, and intake dates change. Everything above was verified in August 2026 and should be re-checked at the source before being planned around — particularly the AccessibleEU annual cycle, the Crisis Centre intake dates, and the FutureLearn course that is not currently running.
