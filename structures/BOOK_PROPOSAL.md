# 📗 Academic Book / Monograph Proposal

> **A complete reference for turning my doctoral work into a book — the proposal that persuades a scholarly press**
> After the PhD, one route for the thesis is a scholarly monograph: a book that reaches beyond examiners to the wider field and, potentially, the public. Presses do not commission from the manuscript alone — they commission from a proposal. This is the document that pitches the book, its argument, its market, and me as its author. It is optional, and career-dependent, but for the social-science and humanities strands of my work it is a genuine possibility worth knowing how to pursue.

## 1. Thesis Is Not a Book

> **My reality:** a thesis and a monograph do different jobs for different readers. Editors know the difference immediately, and a proposal that pitches "my thesis, published" signals that I have not yet made the transition.

A doctoral thesis is written to demonstrate competence to examiners; a monograph is written to make an argument to a field. The transformation is real: the exhaustive literature review shrinks, the methodological defence recedes, the contribution moves to the front, and the prose opens up for a reader who is interested rather than obligated. The proposal must show that I understand this — that I am pitching a book, not repackaging a qualification.

> 💡 **Key question:** Have I described a book that a reader would choose to read — with an argument, a stake, and a reason to exist — rather than a thesis that a candidate was required to write? The editor is looking for the former from the first line.

---

## 2. The Core of the Proposal

Presses vary in their forms, but the persuasive heart of every proposal is the same.

- **The overview** — what the book is about and, above all, what it argues, in a page that makes an editor want to read on
- **The contribution** — what the book adds that the field does not already have; why it matters now
- **The hook** — the single compelling idea that makes this book distinctive and necessary
- **Scope and approach** — what the book covers, how it is built, and the through-line that holds it together

> ⚠️ **Avoid:** Leading with the topic instead of the argument. "This book is about depression care" is a subject. "This book argues that the systems that train practitioners are the neglected key to scaling depression care" is a book. Editors commission arguments.

---

## 3. Market and Audience

A scholarly press is making a commercial as well as an intellectual decision. The proposal must show that a real readership exists.

- **Primary audience** — the specific scholars and students who will read it; the courses it could be assigned on
- **Secondary audiences** — practitioners, policy readers, or the educated general reader, where the work genuinely reaches them
- **Comparable and competing titles** — the recent books in this space, and how mine differs from and improves on them
- **Realistic reach** — neither overclaiming a mass market nor underselling the book's relevance

> 📝 **Tip:** The comparable-titles section is where I prove I know my field as a *market*, not just a literature. Naming the two or three books an editor would think of, and showing precisely how mine is different, is one of the most persuasive things I can do.

---

## 4. The Chapter Outline

The chapter outline is the book made visible — proof that the argument sustains across a whole volume, not just an article.

- A paragraph per chapter: its argument, its role in the whole, and how it follows from the last
- A visible through-line, so the editor sees a book with momentum rather than a set of loosely related papers
- An honest account of what exists in draft and what remains to be written
- A realistic sense of length and completion timeline

> 💡 **Key question:** Reading only my chapter outline, could an editor describe the book's argument from beginning to end? If the outline reads as a list of topics rather than a developing argument, the book has not yet found its spine.

---

## 5. Positioning Myself

The proposal also pitches me — not my whole CV, but my authority to write this particular book.

- The specific expertise and experience that make me the right author for this argument
- My relevant record — the thesis, publications, and public work that establish credibility
- For my profile, the distinctive combination — researcher, practitioner, learning designer, public writer — that lets me write a book others could not
- A brief, honest account of my platform: the audience my public scholarship already reaches, which matters to a press

> 📝 **Tip:** My non-traditional path is an asset here, as elsewhere. A book on accessible depression care written by someone who combines research, clinical practice, accessibility expertise, and a public readership is a more distinctive proposition than the same book from a conventional academic — and editors are alert to distinctiveness.

---

## 6. Choosing a Press

Where I send the proposal matters as much as what it says. Fit is everything.

- Identify presses with a strong, current list in my area — the books I cite are a map to my publishers
- Match ambition to press: a university press, a trade-academic crossover, or a specialist publisher, depending on the book's reach
- Read each press's proposal guidelines and submission policy; some accept simultaneous submissions, some do not
- Approach the right commissioning editor — the person who builds the list my book belongs on

> ⚠️ **Avoid:** Sending one generic proposal to many presses without regard to their lists. An editor can tell instantly whether I know their catalogue, and fit with the list is the first thing they assess.

## Writing Process & Strategy

### ✍️ Recommended Order

| Order | Step | Why |
|-------|------|-----|
| 1 | **Identify the argument the book will make** | Everything follows from the through-line |
| 2 | **Research presses and their lists** | Fit shapes how the proposal is framed |
| 3 | **Draft the chapter outline** | The book, mapped as a developing argument |
| 4 | **Write the overview and contribution** | The persuasive core, written once the shape is clear |
| 5 | **Build the market and comparables section** | Prove the readership and the distinctiveness |
| 6 | **Prepare a polished sample chapter** | Most presses want to see the writing itself |
| 7 | **Tailor to the specific press and editor** | Fit with the list is the first thing assessed |

---

### ✅ Submission Checklist

- [ ] The proposal pitches a book with an argument, not a thesis repackaged
- [ ] The overview makes the argument compelling in its first page
- [ ] The contribution and hook are explicit
- [ ] Primary and secondary audiences are specific and realistic
- [ ] Comparable and competing titles are named, with clear differentiation
- [ ] The chapter outline reads as a developing argument with a through-line
- [ ] Draft status and completion timeline are honest
- [ ] My authority to write this book is established
- [ ] A polished sample chapter is ready if required
- [ ] The press is a genuine fit and its guidelines are followed
- [ ] The proposal is addressed to the right commissioning editor

### 🎓 Notes for My Pathway

This is a genuinely optional output — a conventional academic career does not require a monograph, and much of my contribution may travel further through papers, public scholarship, and practice than through a book. But for the social-science and humanities dimension of my work, a monograph on accessible depression care could carry the argument to an audience no single paper reaches, and could sit alongside a funder like SSHRC that values the book as a form.

If I pursue it, the proposal draws on nearly every capability this repository builds: the argument of my conceptual paper, the evidence of my thesis, the field-command of my reviews, the audience of my public writing, and the self-positioning of my applications. It is, in a sense, where the whole record I have been building becomes a single, sustained statement — should I decide that is the form the work should take.
