# 🎯 Graduate School Application

> **A complete reference for the application materials that get me into the MA and the PhD**
> A graduate application is a portfolio of short, high-stakes documents that must work together to answer one question: *is this person ready, and are they the right fit for us?* For a non-traditional applicant with an unusual profile, these materials do more work than usual — they turn an unconventional path into a coherent, compelling case. This guide covers the statement of intent, the research statement, the academic CV, and the supervisor approach.

## 1. The Application as a Set

> **What I am really being assessed on:** readiness, fit, and the credibility of my trajectory — not just my past marks.

The documents in an application are read together, and they must tell one consistent story. The statement of intent makes the case; the research statement gives it scholarly depth; the CV supplies the evidence; the references corroborate it; the supervisor relationship, at doctoral level, underwrites the whole thing. A contradiction between them — a research interest named in the statement that appears nowhere in the CV — is exactly what a sharp admissions reader notices.

For my profile specifically, the set has a particular job. I am arriving from learning design, fine art, and adjacent master's-level work rather than through a conventional psychology pipeline. The application's task is to make that combination read as *distinctive strength* rather than *poor fit* — to show that the path was deliberate and that it converges on a clear research purpose.

> 💡 **Key question:** If an admissions committee read only my CV, then only my statement, would they meet the same person with the same trajectory? Coherence across the documents is what makes an unusual profile persuasive rather than puzzling.

## 2. Statement of Intent

> **Typical length:** 500–1,000 words (taught) · 1,000–2,000 words (doctoral) — always to the stated limit

The statement of intent (also called statement of purpose, or personal statement) is the single most important document in the application. It is where I explain what I want to study, why, why here, and why me — as a piece of persuasive writing, not a memoir.

---

### 2.1 What It Must Establish

1. **A specific research interest** — named precisely, not gestured at. Not "mental health" but *creative and arts-based approaches to depression, and how they can be made accessible and scaled through the systems that train practitioners*
2. **Why it matters** — the problem and its stakes, grounded in the field, not in personal feeling alone
3. **Why I am prepared** — the specific experiences and skills that make me ready, framed as preparation for this work
4. **Why this programme** — concrete alignment with faculty, resources, or approach; evidence I have researched them
5. **Where it leads** — a credible sense of trajectory beyond the degree

---

### 2.2 Opening and Structure

- Open close to the substance — the problem or the question — not with autobiography or a quotation. The first two sentences should already be about the work
- Move from the problem, to my preparation for it, to the specific fit with this programme, to trajectory
- Every paragraph should earn its place against the word limit; a statement that wanders signals a mind that has not focused its question

> ⚠️ **Avoid:** The origin-story opening ("Ever since I was a child…"). Admissions readers have read thousands. What distinguishes a strong statement is the specificity of the research interest and the evidence of readiness — not the emotional preamble.

---

### 2.3 Naming the Field Precisely

The fastest way to signal readiness is to demonstrate that I already speak the field's language and know its landmarks. In my case, a strong statement names the actual coordinates of the work: the depression treatment gap, the task-shifting literature, and the specific scholars whose work mine sits alongside — Daisy Fancourt on arts and health, Vikram Patel on scalable community delivery. Naming them accurately tells a committee I am not arriving with enthusiasm alone; I am arriving already oriented inside the conversation.

> 📝 **Tip:** Specificity is the whole game. A committee can tell within a paragraph whether an applicant has a real, located research interest or a general area of enthusiasm. The named scholar, the named gap, the named method are what move a statement from promising to compelling.

---

### 2.4 Turning My Profile into an Asset

My non-traditional background is only a liability if I let it read as a detour. Framed correctly, it is the reason my eventual contribution will be one nobody else can make.

- Present the path as convergent: creative practice, learning-systems expertise, and research literacy all bending toward one problem
- Connect each prior chapter to the research interest explicitly — the fine-art practice as a way of knowing that informs creative-health work; the fifteen years of learning design as the systems expertise that makes scaling tractable
- Never apologise for the path; explain it, and let its coherence do the persuading

## 3. Research Statement

> **Required more often at doctoral level; sometimes folded into the statement of intent**

Where the statement of intent persuades, the research statement demonstrates scholarly depth. It is the closest thing to a mini research proposal, and it shows the committee that I can think like a researcher, not just aspire to be one.

---

### 3.1 What It Contains

- **The problem and the gap** — a condensed, critical account of what the field knows and where it stops
- **My research question or direction** — specific and answerable, appropriately scoped to the degree
- **A sense of approach** — the kind of methods the question calls for, and why (detailed at doctoral level, indicative at master's)
- **Fit with the programme and a potential supervisor** — how my direction connects to work already happening here
- **Awareness of what makes the work hard** — a brief, honest acknowledgement of a limitation or tension shows maturity

> 💡 **Key question:** Does my research statement read as a description of a programme already underway — grounded in outputs I have produced — or as a wish for one? Prior work (a conceptual paper, a completed review) is what makes the difference, and it belongs here.

---

### 3.2 The Supervisor Is the Programme

At doctoral level especially, the research statement should make visible that I understand a truth about the PhD: the supervisor matters more than the institution's ranking. The statement should name, or clearly point toward, the faculty member whose work mine aligns with, and articulate the alignment specifically — shared problem, compatible method, complementary expertise.

> 📝 **Tip:** A research statement that could have been sent to any programme is a weak one. The version that names why *this* supervisor, working on *this* problem, is the right home for my question is the one that gets forwarded to that supervisor with a note.

## 4. The Academic CV

The academic CV is not a résumé. It is a structured evidence document, formatted to the conventions of the field, that lets a committee verify the claims my statement makes.

---

### 4.1 Structure and Priorities

- **Education** — degrees, institutions, dates; relevant coursework or thesis titles where they support the application
- **Research outputs** — publications, conference papers, preprints; for me, the conceptual paper and any reviews sit here and carry disproportionate weight
- **Relevant professional experience** — framed for its research and subject relevance, not as a general work history
- **Skills and training** — methods, statistics, languages, certifications (e.g. accessibility credentials, research-methods short courses)
- **Scholarships, awards, and service** — evidence of recognition and contribution

> ⚠️ **Avoid:** A corporate-style CV that foregrounds job duties. An academic committee reads for research potential; professional experience should be translated into that register — what it demonstrates about my capacity to do scholarly work.

---

### 4.2 Making Outputs Visible

For a non-traditional applicant, published or public outputs are the single most persuasive line on the CV. They convert a claimed research interest into demonstrated research activity. A conceptual paper, a systematic review, even a serious body of public scholarship, tells a committee that my agenda is real and that I execute. I place these where they cannot be missed.

## 5. Approaching a Supervisor

At doctoral level, and often for research master's, the application effectively begins before it is submitted — with an email to a prospective supervisor. This is a genre of its own.

---

### 5.1 The Structure of the Email

1. **A specific, honest subject line** — naming the intent (prospective PhD applicant, and the topic)
2. **One sentence on who I am** — enough to place me, no more
3. **The alignment** — the specific overlap between their work and my research direction, showing I have read their actual research, not just their faculty page
4. **What I am asking** — whether they are taking students, and whether they would be open to a short conversation
5. **A brief signal of credibility** — one line pointing to a relevant output or the core of my research agenda
6. **A light, respectful close** — attaching a CV and a short research summary, and leaving the next move to them

> 📝 **Tip:** Concision is respect. A prospective supervisor receives many of these; the ones that get answered are specific, brief, and visibly informed by the person's actual work. A long email that could have been sent to anyone signals the opposite.

---

### 5.2 What the Email Is Really Testing

The supervisor is asking, silently: *would working with this person be rewarding?* Every choice in the email answers that — the precision of the alignment, the quality of the writing, the evidence that I can think independently. The email is a small sample of what a supervisory relationship with me would be like. I write it accordingly.

> ⚠️ **Avoid:** Flattery, over-formality, and vagueness in equal measure. "I am very interested in your fascinating research and would be honoured to work with you" says nothing. "Your 2023 work on X connects directly to a question I am pursuing about Y — I have written a short piece developing it" says everything.

---

## 6. References

References corroborate the story the rest of the application tells. Weak or generic references can undo strong materials.

- Choose referees who can speak specifically to my capacity for the work — ideally those who have seen me think, research, or write
- Brief each referee: give them my statement, my CV, and a short note on what this application needs their letter to establish
- Give them time, and make it easy — the quality of a letter often reflects how well the referee was supported to write it
- Where a conventional academic referee is not available, a referee who can speak credibly to my research capacity and rigour is worth more than a senior name who barely knows my work

## Writing Process & Strategy

### ✍️ Recommended Order

| Order | Step | Why |
|-------|------|-----|
| 1 | **Research programmes and supervisors first** | Fit shapes everything; I cannot write "why here" without knowing here |
| 2 | **Draft the research statement** | Clarifying the scholarly agenda makes the intent statement easier |
| 3 | **Draft the statement of intent** | The persuasive centre, written once the agenda is clear |
| 4 | **Build the academic CV** | Evidence assembled to support the claims just made |
| 5 | **Write supervisor emails** | Sent early, before the formal application where possible |
| 6 | **Brief referees** | Give them the finished materials to write from |
| 7 | **Revise the set for one coherent story** | Read all documents together; fix every contradiction |

---

### ✍️ The Non-Traditional Profile — My Strategy

My whole application strategy rests on one move: building the evidence before I apply. A published conceptual paper and a completed systematic review change how an unconventional profile is read — they turn "unusual applicant hoping to do research" into "researcher already working in this area who now seeks formal training and supervision." The materials describe a programme underway. That is the strongest position a non-traditional applicant can occupy, and it is entirely within my control to build.

---

### ✅ Submission Checklist

- [ ] All documents tell one consistent story with no contradictions
- [ ] Statement of intent names a specific, located research interest
- [ ] The field's actual coordinates are named (the gap, the key scholars, the methods)
- [ ] My non-traditional path is framed as convergent strength, not detour
- [ ] "Why this programme" is concrete and evidence-based
- [ ] Research statement reads as a programme underway, grounded in real outputs
- [ ] The supervisor alignment is specific and correct
- [ ] Academic CV foregrounds research outputs and is formatted to field convention
- [ ] Supervisor emails are brief, specific, and visibly informed by their work
- [ ] Referees are well chosen and fully briefed
- [ ] Every document respects its stated length and format
- [ ] The set has been read as a whole, for coherence, at least once

---

### 🎓 Notes for My Pathway

The vision is explicit that entering the MA *after* the research training and the published thesis changes the application entirely — I arrive with epidemiological literacy, a research profile, and a formed focus, and the statement "writes itself" in a way it could not before. That is the sequencing advantage I am deliberately building: each stage produces the evidence that makes the next application stronger.

For the doctoral application, the supervisor decision is the most important of the whole phase. I begin with relationship-building, not programme ranking — identifying the few faculty whose work sits closest to creative approaches to depression, implementation science, or community-based mental health, and reaching out before I apply. A supervisor who wants me matters more than a highly ranked programme that does not.
