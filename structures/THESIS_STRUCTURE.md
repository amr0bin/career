## 1. Introduction

> **Typical length:** 5–10% of total word count
> *(MSc: ~1,000–1,500 words · PhD: ~3,000–5,000 words)*

The introduction is my **contract with the reader**. By the end of it they should know exactly what problem I am studying, why it matters, and what specific question my thesis will answer. It is also the section most examiners read first *and* last — so it needs to be tight, confident, and precise.

---

### 1.1 Rationale

This is my opening argument for why this research needs to exist. Start broad — establish the real-world or theoretical context — then funnel down to the specific problem I am addressing. Think of it as the answer to: *So what?*

- Open with a compelling observation, statistic, or tension in the field
- Establish the significance: who is affected, what is at stake, why now?
- Avoid inflated claims — *"this is the most important issue of our time"* signals weak argumentation
- End this subsection pointing clearly toward the gap my study addresses

> 💡 **Key question:** If a non-specialist asked *"why does this matter?"*, could I answer in two sentences?

---

### 1.2 Structure Overview

A brief roadmap of what the reader will encounter in each chapter. This is functional, not decorative — it helps examiners navigate a long document and signals that my logic is ordered.

- One to two sentences per chapter is sufficient
- Write this **last**, after I know what each chapter actually contains
- Use consistent, precise language that mirrors my actual chapter headings

> 📝 **Tip:** Write a placeholder here early, then revise it once my full draft is complete. Many students find the structure shifts during writing.

---

### 1.3 Research Question

The most important sentence in my thesis. Everything I write, in every section, should be in service of this question. It must be specific, answerable with the methods I am using, and appropriately scoped.

- One primary research question is standard; secondary sub-questions are acceptable if needed
- A good research question **cannot** be answered with a simple yes or no
- It should not be so broad that a literature review would answer it, or so narrow that it becomes trivial
- If I have hypotheses (quantitative work), state them here

> 💡 **Key question:** Can I identify which section of my thesis answers this question? That section is my Results or Findings — everything else supports it.

> ⚠️ **Avoid:** The most common introduction mistake is burying the research question at the end of a long literature survey. State it clearly and early — by the end of section 1.3.

## 2. Literature Review

> **Typical length:** 20–30% of total word count
> *(MSc: ~4,000–6,000 words · PhD: ~12,000–20,000 words)*

The literature review is **not** a summary of everything I have read. It is a structured critical argument that maps existing knowledge, traces its development, evaluates its strengths and weaknesses, and locates precisely where my study sits within — and contributes to — that body of knowledge.

---

### 2.1 Theoretical Framework

Identify the theoretical lens or lenses through which I am examining my topic. This is the conceptual architecture that shapes what questions I ask, what I consider relevant data, and how I interpret what I find.

- Name and explain the theory or theories I am working within
- Justify why this framework is appropriate for *my question* — not just because it is popular
- In social science, disability studies, counselling, and clinical psych, this section is often substantial and deeply contested — engage with that critically
- If using multiple frameworks (e.g. intersectionality + critical realism), explain how they relate to each other

> 📝 **Tip:** My theoretical framework will determine my methodology. A reader should be able to predict my methods from reading this section. If there is a mismatch, revise one or the other.

---

### 2.2 State of the Art

Survey the existing research relevant to my topic. This is not a book-by-book or article-by-article summary — it is a **thematic synthesis**. Group studies by what they find, debate, or assume, and evaluate them critically.

- Organise **thematically**, not chronologically (unless the history of the field is my argument)
- Evaluate quality: note sample sizes, methodological limitations, context-specificity
- Show I understand what is **contested**, not just what is established
- Cite primarily peer-reviewed work; where grey literature is appropriate (policy docs, clinical guidelines), justify its inclusion
- Use databases suited to my field: PsycINFO, PubMed, CINAHL, Social Care Online, JSTOR

> ⚠️ **Avoid:** The "annotated bibliography" trap — listing what each author said without synthesising across sources. My voice should organise the literature, not the other way around.

---

### 2.3 Gap Statement

The most critical paragraph of the literature review. It is the logical culmination of everything I have mapped above. Based on what the field has and has not done, I now establish the specific, justified gap my thesis addresses.

- The gap should **follow directly** from my critical evaluation — not appear as a surprise
- Be precise: a gap is not *"no one has studied this"* — it is *"this mechanism has not been studied in this population / using this method / within this theoretical frame"*
- The gap statement leads directly into — and should mirror — my research question

> 💡 **Key question:** Does my gap statement make the reader think *"of course — this is exactly what needs investigating next"*? That is the standard I am aiming for.

## 3. Methodology

> **Typical length:** 15–20% of total word count
> *(MSc: ~2,500–4,000 words · PhD: ~8,000–15,000 words)*

The methodology chapter is my **scholarly transparency**. It tells the reader exactly how I investigated my question, and — crucially — *why* those choices were the right ones. An examiner should be able to replicate my study from this chapter alone.

---

### 3.1 Design

State my overall research design: quantitative, qualitative, or mixed methods. Identify the specific design type within that category.

| Type | Examples |
|------|---------|
| **Qualitative** | Thematic analysis, grounded theory, phenomenology, discourse analysis, case study, ethnography |
| **Quantitative** | RCT, survey, longitudinal cohort, cross-sectional, quasi-experimental |
| **Mixed** | Specify the integration strategy: concurrent, sequential, or embedded |

Also describe my **epistemological position** (interpretivist, positivist, constructivist, critical realist) — this connects directly back to my theoretical framework.

> 📝 **Tip:** In Disability Studies and Counselling Psychology, the design and epistemological position are often the heart of the scholarly contribution. Do not treat them as administrative boxes to tick.

---

### 3.2 Method

The step-by-step account of what I actually did. Be precise — this section should have enough detail that another researcher could replicate my study.

- **Participants / sample:** who, how many, how recruited, inclusion and exclusion criteria
- **Data collection:** instruments used (interview schedule, survey, observation protocol), how administered, where, when
- **Ethical considerations:** ethics approval, consent process, anonymisation, right to withdraw
- **Data analysis:** the specific procedure used to move from raw data to findings (e.g. Braun & Clarke's six-phase thematic analysis; SPSS procedure; NVivo coding protocol)

---

### 3.3 Justification

Defend every significant methodological choice. Do not just describe what I did — explain why it was the best available option for my question.

- Why this design rather than an alternative?
- Why this sample size / sampling strategy?
- Why this specific analytic approach?
- Cite **methodological literature** — not just substantive literature — to support my choices

> 💡 **Key question:** For every method choice, ask: *"What is the alternative, and why is my choice better suited to my question?"* That reasoning belongs here.

---

### 3.4 Limitations

Acknowledge the known constraints of my approach honestly and analytically. This is not weakness — it is scholarly rigour.

- **Scope limitations:** what my study cannot generalise to
- **Method limitations:** what my chosen design cannot capture
- **Practical limitations:** sample access, time, resources
- For each limitation, briefly note how I mitigated it or why it does not invalidate my findings

> ⚠️ **Avoid:** Do not over-apologise for limitations, and do not hide them. Frame them as the honest boundary conditions of my contribution — this is what experienced researchers do.

---

## 4. Results

> **Typical length:** 15–25% of total word count
> *Length varies greatly by method and data volume.*

Results is the most **rule-governed** section of my thesis. Its single job is to present what I found, organised in a way that directly maps to my research question and my methods. **Interpretation belongs in Discussion — not here.**

---

### 4.1 Presenting My Data

The organisation of my results should mirror the logic of my research question and the structure of my analysis.

| Method | How to present |
|--------|---------------|
| **Qualitative** | Present themes or categories with illustrative quotes. Each theme needs: a label, a description, and evidence from my data |
| **Quantitative** | Present statistics in logical order (descriptive first, then inferential). Use tables and figures, but narrate what they show — do not simply dump numbers |
| **Mixed methods** | Report qualitative and quantitative results separately before integrating |

**The cardinal rule:** Every result I report must connect to my research question. If a finding does not relate to my question, it does not belong in Results.

> ⚠️ **Avoid:** *"Participants reported high levels of anxiety"* is a result. *"This suggests the intervention was insufficient"* is an interpretation — it belongs in Discussion.

> 📝 **Tip:** Structure my Results section using my themes or sub-questions as subheadings. This creates a clear audit trail from question → findings → discussion.

---

## 5. Discussion

> **Typical length:** 15–20% of total word count
> *Often the most intellectually demanding section to write.*

Discussion is where my **scholarly voice is loudest**. This is where I interpret what my results mean, connect them back to the literature I reviewed, explore what was surprising, and articulate what my findings actually contribute to the field.

---

### 5.1 Interpretation, Literature Links, and Implications

Work through my results systematically. For each major finding:

1. **Interpret** — what does this finding mean within my theoretical framework?
2. **Link** — how does it confirm, challenge, extend, or contradict the existing literature?
3. **Imply** — what are the broader implications: theoretical, clinical, policy, social?

Also use this section to:
- Address **unexpected or negative findings** — these are often my most interesting contributions
- Revisit my theoretical framework and reflect on whether findings support, complicate, or require revision of it
- Note the boundaries of my interpretation — what I *cannot* claim from my data

> 💡 **Key question:** For every result, ask: *"Why does this matter beyond my own study?"* That answer is my contribution to the field.

> 📝 **Tip:** In Disability Studies and Counselling Psych especially, the Discussion is the place for **reflexivity** — examining how my positionality shaped my interpretations. This is not optional in these fields; it is a mark of sophistication.

> ⚠️ **Avoid:** Do not simply repeat my results in the Discussion. Each paragraph should advance the interpretive argument, not recap the data.

## 6. Conclusion

> **Typical length:** 3–7% of total word count
> *(MSc: ~600–1,000 words · PhD: ~2,000–4,000 words)*

The conclusion closes the contract I opened in the Introduction. It directly answers my research question, states my original contribution to knowledge, and opens the horizon for future research. It should feel like an **arrival** — not a summary.

---

### 6.1 Answer, Contributions, and Future Directions

Structure my conclusion around three moves:

1. **Answer the research question** — State directly and explicitly what my thesis found. Do not hedge excessively — I have earned the right to make a claim.

2. **State my contributions** — What does this thesis add that was not there before? This might be:
   - *Empirical* — new data
   - *Theoretical* — a new framework or modification
   - *Methodological* — a new approach
   - *Practical* — evidence for a new intervention or policy

3. **Future directions** — What questions does my work open up? What should researchers investigate next? What would I do differently or extend if I continued?

> 💡 **Key question:** Can I finish this sentence clearly and confidently: *"This thesis demonstrates that…"*? That sentence is my conclusion's core.

> ⚠️ **Avoid:** Do not introduce new evidence or new arguments in the Conclusion. If something belongs here, it should have appeared in Results or Discussion first.

> 📝 **Tip:** The Introduction and Conclusion should read as a matched pair. A reader who reads only those two sections should understand exactly what I studied, why, and what I found.

## Writing Process & Strategy

### ✍️ Recommended Writing Order

Counterintuitively, the order I *write* a thesis is not the order it is *read*. Most experienced academic writers use this sequence:

| Order | Section | Why |
|-------|---------|-----|
| 1 | **Research Question** | Everything else is built on this. Refine it first. |
| 2 | **Methodology** | Write while doing the research — capture decisions in real time. |
| 3 | **Results** | Systematic and descriptive — write while the data is freshest. |
| 4 | **Literature Review** | I now know what literature is actually relevant to my findings. |
| 5 | **Discussion** | Requires Results and Literature Review to be substantially complete. |
| 6 | **Introduction** | Write last — I now know exactly what my thesis is about. |
| 7 | **Conclusion** | Write after Introduction — mirror and close the contract. |
| 8 | **Abstract** | Write very last — it summarises a complete work. |

---

### ✅ Submission Checklist

Use before submitting any chapter draft, and again for the full thesis.

- [ ] Research question is stated clearly in the Introduction and answered directly in the Conclusion
- [ ] Every chapter connects logically to the one before and after it
- [ ] Literature Review synthesises thematically — not source by source
- [ ] Gap statement follows from the critical evaluation of literature
- [ ] Methodology justifies every major design choice with methodological literature
- [ ] Results section contains no interpretation (interpretation is in Discussion only)
- [ ] Every result links back to the research question
- [ ] Discussion connects each finding to the existing literature
- [ ] Limitations are acknowledged with mitigation noted
- [ ] Conclusion directly states the answer to the research question
- [ ] Original contributions are explicitly named in the Conclusion
- [ ] Introduction and Conclusion read as a coherent matched pair
- [ ] Abstract is written last and covers: aim, methods, findings, conclusions
- [ ] References are complete, consistently formatted, and include all cited works
- [ ] Word count is within the range specified by my institution

### 🎓 Notes for Specific Degrees

#### MSc (e.g. Disability Studies)
Emphasis on mastery of existing knowledge and rigorous application of a method. Original empirical contribution is valued but less expected than at PhD level. Theoretical sophistication and critical engagement with literature are central markers of a strong thesis.

#### MA (e.g. Counselling Psychology)
The Discussion section typically carries extra weight — clinical reflection, ethical reasoning, and reflexivity about my own positionality are frequently assessed. My theoretical framework is often deeply personalised and must be clearly justified.

#### Diploma (e.g. Art Therapy)
Practitioner-research integration is key. How does my empirical work relate to practice? My Limitations section often includes explicit reflexive discussion of my dual role as practitioner and researcher.

#### PhD (e.g. Clinical Psych / Public Health / Social Science)
Original contribution to knowledge is **mandatory** — not aspirational. My gap statement, theoretical framework, and Discussion must clearly articulate what the field now knows that it did not before my thesis. Examiners will ask directly: *"What is new here?"*
