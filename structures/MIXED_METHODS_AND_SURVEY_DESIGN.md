# 📋 Mixed-Methods Design & Survey Instrument Development

> **How to design the pilot: a survey of process features with validated measures, explained by interviews.**
> The Leeds Applied Project and the MA thesis are explanatory sequential mixed-methods studies. Two crafts carry them: building a new instrument (the process-feature survey) and integrating two strands so that each explains the other.

---

## 1. The structure

| Part | What it does | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Design statement | Which mixed-methods design and why | Explanatory sequential: survey, then interviews |
| Constructs and measures | Process features (new instrument) and outcomes (validated scales) | Wellbeing, depressive symptoms, help-seeking |
| Instrument development | Item writing, expert review, cognitive interviews, pilot | Disabled co-researcher reviews every item |
| Sampling and recruitment | By experience of the process, not by diagnosis | Through a DPO or NEADS |
| Qualitative strand | Who is interviewed, chosen from survey results | Purposive selection |
| Integration | Where and how the strands meet | A joint display table |
| Accessibility of the instrument | Formats, plain language, breaks, assistive technology | The instrument must not reproduce the barrier it studies |

---

## 2. How to do it well

1. Name the design using Creswell & Plano Clark's typology in the first paragraph.
2. Write process-feature items from the policy analysis (documentation asked, waits, re-proving, fit with fluctuation).
3. Run cognitive interviews with five to eight people to test whether items are understood as intended.
4. Choose validated outcomes with Canadian use and free licences; report reliability in your sample.
5. Plan integration in advance: a joint display linking each survey pattern to the interview themes that explain it.
6. Read clinical scales as measures of what the barrier costs, not properties of the student (thesis-line condition 2).

### 📚 Credible examples

- Boateng, G. O., et al. (2018). Best practices for developing and validating scales for health, social, and behavioral research. *Frontiers in Public Health, 6*, 149.
- Willis, G. B. (2005). *Cognitive Interviewing: A Tool for Improving Questionnaire Design*. Sage.
- Outcome measures with wide use: Tennant, R., et al. (2007). The Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-being Scale (WEMWBS). *Health and Quality of Life Outcomes, 5*, 63; Kroenke, K., Spitzer, R. L., & Williams, J. B. W. (2001). The PHQ-9. *Journal of General Internal Medicine, 16*(9), 606–613.
- Reporting: GRAMMS (O'Cathain, Murphy & Nicholl, 2008, *Journal of Health Services Research & Policy, 13*(2), 92–98).

### ⚡ Do it well and fast

- Draft items in Leeds Year 1 from the policy essay; pilot them in the Applied Project; refine them for the MA.
- Use one data dictionary across pilot and MA so the datasets can be compared.
