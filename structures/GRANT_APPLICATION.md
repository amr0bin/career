# 💰 Grant & Fellowship Application

> **A complete reference for research funding applications — CIHR, SSHRC, Wellcome, and beyond**
> A grant application is the most demanding test of research literacy there is. It asks me to convince expert reviewers, in a fixed number of pages, that I understand the evidence, have found a genuine gap, hold a credible plan to address it, and am the person to carry it out. It is also the practical skill that makes an independent research programme possible — and one I can begin practising early, through small fellowships and studentships.

## 1. What Reviewers Are Actually Scoring

> **My reality:** most applications are rejected. A fundable application is not one without weaknesses — it is one whose strengths are unmistakable and whose weaknesses are anticipated and managed.

Every funding scheme dresses its criteria in its own language, but underneath, reviewers are answering three questions. If my application does not answer all three with confidence, it will not be funded.

| The reviewer's question | What convinces them |
|-------------------------|---------------------|
| **Is this worth doing?** (Significance) | A real problem, a genuine gap, and a clear account of what changes if the work succeeds |
| **Will this approach work?** (Approach) | A design that fits the question, is technically sound, and shows I have anticipated what could go wrong |
| **Can *this person / team* deliver it?** (Feasibility) | Evidence of the skills, environment, support, and realism to complete what I propose |

> 💡 **Key question:** A tired reviewer with ten applications to score reads my summary and my significance section closely and skims the rest. Do those two pieces alone make the case? If the argument only emerges on page eight, it will not be found.

## 2. The Core Components

Funders vary, but most research grant and fellowship applications assemble the same parts. I write to the specific scheme's structure, but I prepare these building blocks.

- **Summary / abstract** — the whole case in miniature; often the only part every reviewer reads in full
- **Background and rationale** — the state of the evidence, the gap, and why the work matters now
- **Aims / research questions / objectives** — specific, bounded, and mapped to the approach
- **Approach / methodology** — design, participants or data, analysis, and rigour
- **Significance and impact** — the difference the work makes, and to whom
- **Knowledge translation / dissemination** — how the findings reach the people who can use them
- **Team and environment** — who is involved and why the setting can support the work
- **Budget and justification** — every cost, defended
- **Timeline** — a realistic, phased plan with milestones

> 📝 **Tip:** I write the summary last but treat it as the most important 200 words in the document. It sets the frame through which every other section is read.

## 3. The Case for Support

The narrative heart of the application is the case for support. It stands or falls on three pillars.

---

### 3.1 Significance

Significance is not a claim that the topic is important in general — it is a precise account of what my specific work will change.

- Establish the scale of the problem with evidence (for my field: the depression treatment gap, in the epidemiological terms reviewers expect — prevalence, burden, treatment coverage)
- Name the gap exactly: not "more research is needed" but *this mechanism, in this population, using this approach, has not been examined*
- State the payoff concretely: what will the field, practice, or policy be able to do after this work that it cannot do now?

> ⚠️ **Avoid:** Significance sections built on sweeping assertion ("mental health is a global crisis"). Reviewers in this field know the statistics better than I do. What persuades them is the precision of the gap and the credibility of the contribution, grounded in the literature.

---

### 3.2 Approach

Approach is where feasibility and rigour are demonstrated. This is the section that separates fundable applications from hopeful ones.

- Match the design to the question — the right method, not the most elaborate one. For an early-stage question, a feasibility study is often more fundable than an underpowered trial
- Show the reasoning behind each design choice, and name the alternative I rejected and why
- Address rigour explicitly: for quantitative work, power and analysis plan; for qualitative work, the specific analytic approach and how I ensure trustworthiness
- Include a risks-and-mitigations passage — reviewers trust an applicant who has already imagined what could go wrong

> 💡 **Key question:** Could a methods reviewer predict my analysis plan from my aims? If the path from question to method to analysis is not visibly connected, the approach reads as aspiration rather than design.

---

### 3.3 Feasibility

Feasibility is the quiet criterion that sinks strong ideas. Reviewers ask whether *this* applicant, in *this* environment, can actually deliver.

- Evidence of relevant skills — and, where I lack a skill, the named collaborator who holds it (a biostatistician for complex analysis, a local partner for community access)
- A realistic timeline with milestones, weighted honestly toward analysis and writing, not just data collection
- The strength of the environment: supervision, institutional support, access to data or populations
- Preliminary work that de-risks the proposal — a published conceptual paper, a completed systematic review, a pilot

> 📝 **Tip:** For a non-traditional applicant, feasibility is where prior outputs do the heaviest lifting. A published paper and a completed review are the most persuasive evidence that my research programme is real and that I can execute it.

## 4. The Funder-Specific Lens

The same project is framed differently for different funders. Framing for more than one agency widens the funding surface substantially.

| Funder | What it funds and how to frame for it |
|--------|--------------------------------------|
| **CIHR** (Canadian Institutes of Health Research) | The primary federal health-research funder. Frame the work in health-outcomes and health-systems terms; emphasise clinical and population significance. Small fellowships and studentships are the place to practise the genre early |
| **SSHRC** (Social Sciences and Humanities Research Council) | Frames around social, cultural, and humanistic contribution. My creative-health and arts-based work sits legitimately here — foreground meaning, experience, and the arts-and-society dimension |
| **Wellcome Trust** | Has made mental health a strategic priority and funds innovative, sometimes non-traditional approaches. Strong fit for the international dimension and for work in low- and middle-income contexts |

- The health-and-humanities intersection means my work can often be framed for *either* CIHR or SSHRC — I decide which agency's reviewers I most want, and lead with their priorities
- Read recently funded abstracts where they are public; they reveal what each panel actually rewards
- Align every part of the application with the scheme's stated assessment criteria, in the scheme's own order

> ⚠️ **Avoid:** Submitting one generic narrative to multiple funders. Each agency has a distinct mission; an application that ignores it signals that I have not done my homework.

---

## 5. Budget and Non-Narrative Parts

The parts that are not the argument still decide the outcome. Reviewers read carelessness in the budget as carelessness in the science.

- **Budget:** every line justified, proportionate, and clearly tied to an activity in the plan. Neither padded nor implausibly lean
- **Justification:** a short reason for each cost — reviewers must see *why* each item is needed, not just *what* it is
- **Data management / ethics:** a credible plan for consent, storage, and — where relevant — the ethics of research with vulnerable populations and across international partnerships
- **Formatting compliance:** page limits, fonts, and section order are non-negotiable; applications are returned unread for breaching them

> 📝 **Tip:** I read the ethics and equity dimensions of my international work as substance, not paperwork. Reviewers in global mental health are alert to extractive North–South dynamics; showing I have thought about equitable partnership is part of the scientific case, not an add-on.

## Writing Process & Strategy

### ✍️ Recommended Order

| Order | Step | Why |
|-------|------|-----|
| 1 | **Read the scheme's criteria and structure in full** | The application is written *to* the criteria, in their order |
| 2 | **Draft aims and approach** | The scientific core everything else supports |
| 3 | **Draft significance** | Written once I know exactly what the approach delivers |
| 4 | **Build budget, timeline, and team** | The feasibility case, assembled deliberately |
| 5 | **Draft knowledge translation and impact** | How the work travels beyond the grant |
| 6 | **Write the summary last** | The whole case distilled into its most-read paragraph |
| 7 | **Seek review from someone who has held the grant** | An insider reading is worth more than ten self-edits |

---

### ✍️ On Resubmission

Most funded grants were funded on resubmission. A rejection with reviewer comments is data, not a verdict.

- Read every comment as a signal of where the argument did not land
- Respond to each substantive point in the revision, and say what I changed
- Treat the first submission as the beginning of a conversation with the panel, not a single shot

---

### ✅ Submission Checklist

- [ ] The application is written to the scheme's exact criteria, in their order
- [ ] Summary makes the entire case on its own
- [ ] Significance names a precise gap and a concrete payoff, grounded in evidence
- [ ] Approach matches the question and shows the reasoning behind each choice
- [ ] Risks and mitigations are addressed
- [ ] Feasibility is evidenced — skills, collaborators, environment, prior outputs
- [ ] Timeline is realistic and weighted toward analysis and writing
- [ ] Budget is complete, proportionate, and every line justified
- [ ] Ethics, data management, and partnership equity are addressed
- [ ] Knowledge translation shows how findings reach those who can use them
- [ ] Funder mission and framing are aligned (CIHR / SSHRC / Wellcome)
- [ ] All formatting and page-limit rules are met exactly
- [ ] The application has been read by someone experienced with the scheme

### 🎓 Notes for My Pathway

I do not need to wait for the doctorate to start. Graduate fellowships, studentships, and small project grants are where I learn the genre at low stakes — and each application, funded or not, sharpens the articulation of my research programme. The discipline of writing to reviewer criteria is itself a form of research literacy: it forces me to demonstrate that I understand the evidence base, can locate a genuine gap, and hold the methods to address it.

The conceptual paper and the systematic review are the preliminary work that makes my early grant applications credible. By the time I write a substantial application, the case for feasibility is already partly built — I am not promising I can do research, I am pointing to research I have already done.
