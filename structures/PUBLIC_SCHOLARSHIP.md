## 1. What Public Scholarship Is For

> **My format:** typically 800–2,000 words per piece · a sustainable regular rhythm · a named practitioner-and-public audience

Academic publication and public scholarship do different jobs, and I need both. A peer-reviewed paper establishes a claim to the field's satisfaction; it is precise, slow, and read by specialists. A public essay does what the paper cannot — it carries the idea to the practitioner who will use it, the person who lives with the condition, the funder deciding where attention goes. This is knowledge translation: not simplification, but *transmission*.

For my portfolio specifically, the public writing — the Accessible Learning Labs Substack — is the connective tissue. It builds an audience before I have credentials to trade on. It forces me to clarify half-formed ideas into public prose, which is the fastest way to find out whether an idea is real. And it accumulates: unlike a single paper, a body of public work compounds in reach and authority with every piece.

> 💡 **Key question:** After reading one piece, would a practitioner understand something they can *act on* — and want to read the next one? Public scholarship earns its audience one useful idea at a time.

## 2. The One-Idea Discipline

The most common failure of public writing is trying to say everything. A strong piece says one thing well.

- Each piece carries a single idea that can be stated in one sentence before I begin
- Everything in the piece serves that idea; anything that does not, however interesting, belongs in a different piece
- The discipline is subtractive — the essay improves as I remove what does not serve the one idea

> 📝 **Tip:** If I cannot name the one idea in a sentence, the piece is not ready to draft. "The training system is as important as the intervention" is one idea. "Everything I think about depression and creativity and access" is a newsletter's worth of ideas competing in one post — and losing.

## 3. Structure of a Piece

Public writing rewards a clear, generous structure — one that respects the reader's time and never makes them work to find the point.

---

### 3.1 The Shape

1. **The hook** — open with the concrete: a moment, a fact, a tension the reader recognises. Never open with abstraction or throat-clearing
2. **The turn** — name the idea the piece will deliver, early. Public readers decide fast whether to keep going; I earn the continuation by showing the payoff quickly
3. **The development** — the idea, built out with evidence, example, and reasoning, in plain language
4. **The stakes** — why this matters for the reader specifically, and what it changes
5. **The close** — land the one idea cleanly; leave the reader with something they can carry

> ⚠️ **Avoid:** The academic reflex of withholding the point until the end. In a journal, the argument can unfold slowly. In public writing, burying the idea past the first few paragraphs loses the reader before they reach it.

---

### 3.2 Voice

Public scholarship has a voice that academic writing suppresses — direct, present, and unafraid of the first person. I write as a thinking practitioner talking to another, not as an institution addressing a discipline.

- Short sentences carry the weight; long ones are earned, not habitual
- Concrete beats abstract every time — the specific example does more than the general claim
- Plain verbs, active voice, no filler; every sentence does one job

---

## 4. Translating Evidence Honestly

The integrity of public scholarship rests on getting the evidence right while making it reachable. Simplification is legitimate; distortion is not.

- Represent the evidence accurately, including its limits — where the arts-and-depression evidence is promising but contested, I say so, because credibility is the whole asset
- Translate the finding, not the jargon: what a study *means* for a practitioner, not its confidence intervals
- Distinguish clearly between what the evidence establishes and what I am arguing beyond it
- Cite and link generously — sending readers to primary sources builds trust and models good practice

> 💡 **Key question:** Would the researcher whose work I am translating recognise their finding in my description of it, and be comfortable with how I have framed its certainty? If not, I have simplified into distortion.

> ⚠️ **Avoid:** Overclaiming for effect. Public writing tempts me toward the bold, tidy claim. But my long-term credibility — the thing that makes the audience valuable — depends on the audience being able to trust that I never oversell the evidence.

## 5. Building a Body of Work

A single post is a piece of writing. A sustained body of public work is an asset — and the asset is the point.

- Consistency compounds: a regular rhythm, sustained over years, builds reach and authority that no single piece can
- Themes accumulate into a recognisable body of thought; over time the work maps a distinctive intellectual territory that is unmistakably mine
- The public work and the formal research feed each other — a public essay can be the first articulation of an idea that later becomes a conceptual paper; a published paper can become an accessible essay that carries it further
- Each piece is a small, low-stakes rehearsal of the thinking that the higher-stakes outputs require

> 📝 **Tip:** The compounding only works if the rhythm is sustainable. A modest cadence I can hold for years beats an ambitious one I abandon in months. I define the minimum viable rhythm and protect it, rather than over-committing and stalling.

## Writing Process & Strategy

### ✍️ Recommended Order

| Order | Step | Why |
|-------|------|-----|
| 1 | **Name the one idea** | If I cannot state it in a sentence, I am not ready to write |
| 2 | **Find the concrete hook** | The specific entry point that earns the reader's attention |
| 3 | **Draft fast, in my own voice** | Public writing dies under over-editing; get it down |
| 4 | **Check the evidence** | Confirm every claim is accurate and fairly framed |
| 5 | **Cut to the one idea** | Remove everything that does not serve it |
| 6 | **Read it aloud** | The ear catches what the eye misses; public prose must sound like a person |

---

### ✍️ Ethics and Boundaries

Public writing about depression and clinical work carries real responsibility.

- Never breach confidentiality — no identifiable clients, no borrowed private material, ever
- Write about vulnerable experience with care; the audience includes people living with what I describe
- Where I write from clinical or facilitation experience, protect the people in those rooms absolutely
- Distinguish clearly between my professional view and established consensus, so a reader is never misled about what carries the field's weight

---

### ✅ Submission Checklist

- [ ] The piece carries one idea, statable in a sentence
- [ ] It opens with the concrete, not the abstract
- [ ] The payoff is visible early — the reader knows why to continue
- [ ] The evidence is accurate, and its limits are honestly represented
- [ ] Claims are distinguished from established consensus
- [ ] Primary sources are linked and credited
- [ ] The voice is direct, plain, and human — read aloud and confirmed
- [ ] No confidentiality is breached; vulnerable experience is handled with care
- [ ] Everything that does not serve the one idea has been cut
- [ ] The cadence is one I can actually sustain

### 🎓 Notes for My Pathway

The vision names the Substack as the highest-compounding professional asset I have, and treats it as public scholarship rather than a side project — the public face of the research record, doing the knowledge-translation work that academic publication cannot. I hold it to that standard: not casual blogging, but serious writing that happens to be reachable.

Practically, it is also where my ideas are first tested. The argument that becomes the conceptual paper can be rehearsed here first; the findings of the systematic review can be carried to a practitioner audience here after publication. The public work and the formal work are one body of thought expressed at two altitudes — and both compound.
