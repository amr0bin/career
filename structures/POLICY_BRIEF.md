# 📊 Policy Brief & Knowledge Mobilization

> **A complete reference for turning evidence into decisions — the output that reaches people with power to change things**
> A policy brief translates research into a short, decision-focused document for people who act on evidence but do not have time to read the studies — commissioners, service leaders, funders, government. It is where my research stops being a contribution to a literature and becomes a lever on the real-world problem I care about: the accessibility of depression care.

## 1. What a Policy Brief Is For

> **My format:** typically 2–4 pages · one problem · a small set of clear recommendations · written for action, not for the record

A policy brief exists to change a decision. Its reader is not a scholar evaluating my rigour but a decision-maker asking *what should I do, and why should I believe you?* Everything about the genre follows from that: it leads with the recommendation, not the literature review; it is short because attention is scarce; and it is judged by whether it moves someone to act, not by its comprehensiveness.

This output matters to my whole purpose. My research is ultimately about closing the depression treatment gap — and gaps close through decisions made by people who allocate resources. The policy brief is how my evidence reaches them. It is knowledge mobilization in its most direct form.

> 💡 **Key question:** After reading my brief, does a busy decision-maker know exactly what I am asking them to do, why it matters, and that the evidence supports it? If any of those three is unclear, the brief has not done its job.

## 2. Knowing My Audience

The single most important move is to write for the specific person who can act, in the terms that matter to them.

- Identify the actual decision-maker — a commissioner, a service director, a funder, a policy team — and what they control
- Frame the problem in *their* terms: cost, reach, outcomes, risk, equity — whichever is the currency of their decisions
- Respect their expertise and their time; they know their world, and the brief earns trust by being useful, not by lecturing

> ⚠️ **Avoid:** Writing an academic paper in miniature. A decision-maker does not need my methodology defended or the literature surveyed. They need the finding, its reliability, and what to do about it — in that order.

---

## 3. The Structure

A policy brief inverts the academic order: the answer comes first, and the support follows for those who want it.

| Element | What it does |
|---------|-------------|
| **Title** | Names the problem and hints at the recommendation — concrete, not clever |
| **Executive summary** | The whole brief in a few sentences: problem, finding, recommendation |
| **The problem** | Why this matters now, in the audience's terms, with the key evidence |
| **The evidence** | What the research shows, briefly and accessibly — the reliability, not the methodology |
| **Recommendations** | A small number of specific, feasible actions (see §4) |
| **Implications / next steps** | What acting — or not acting — leads to |

- Lead with the summary; many readers read only that, so it must stand alone
- Keep the evidence section proportionate — enough to be credible, never enough to overwhelm
- Use the audience's language throughout, not the field's jargon

> 📝 **Tip:** The executive summary is the brief. I write it to work entirely on its own, because for many of my readers it will be the only part that gets read — and it still needs to move them.

---

## 4. Recommendations That Land

Recommendations are where a brief succeeds or fails. Vague or unrealistic recommendations get ignored; specific, feasible ones get acted on.

- **Specific:** name the concrete action, not a direction of travel ("fund X training in Y settings," not "invest in workforce development")
- **Feasible:** within the reader's actual power and resources; a recommendation they cannot enact is wasted
- **Costed and consequenced, where I can:** what it takes, and what it returns
- **Few:** a small number of strong recommendations beats a long list that diffuses attention

> 💡 **Key question:** Could the person reading this actually do what I am recommending, with the levers they hold? If the recommendation sits outside their power, I am writing to the wrong reader — or asking the wrong thing.

---

## 5. Design and Accessibility

A policy brief is a visual document, and its design is part of its argument. This is squarely my expertise, and I hold my own briefs to the standard I would set for anyone.

- Clear visual hierarchy — the reader's eye should find the summary and recommendations without effort
- One or two well-chosen visuals that carry meaning, not decoration
- Genuine accessibility — legible type, sufficient contrast, plain language, structure that works for every reader
- Restraint: a clean, professional brief is more persuasive than a busy one

> 📝 **Tip:** My accessibility and learning-design background is a real advantage here. Most researchers produce briefs that are cramped and hard to read; a genuinely accessible, well-structured brief is both more effective and a quiet demonstration of the exact expertise my research is built on.

## Writing Process & Strategy

### ✍️ Recommended Order

| Order | Step | Why |
|-------|------|-----|
| 1 | **Identify the decision-maker and the decision** | Everything is written to them and to it |
| 2 | **Draft the recommendations** | The point of the brief; write them first and build backward |
| 3 | **Write the problem in their terms** | Framed in the currency of their decisions |
| 4 | **Distil the evidence** | Credible, brief, accessible |
| 5 | **Write the executive summary last** | The whole brief, made to stand alone |
| 6 | **Design for accessibility and hierarchy** | The format is part of the persuasion |

---

### ✅ Submission Checklist

- [ ] The specific decision-maker and decision are identified
- [ ] The problem is framed in the audience's terms, not the field's
- [ ] The executive summary works entirely on its own
- [ ] Evidence is credible, brief, and jargon-free
- [ ] Recommendations are specific, feasible, and few
- [ ] Recommendations sit within the reader's actual power
- [ ] Consequences of acting and not acting are clear
- [ ] The design has clear hierarchy and genuine accessibility
- [ ] Visuals carry meaning rather than decorate
- [ ] The brief could plausibly move someone to act

### 🎓 Notes for My Pathway

The policy brief connects my research to the change it is ultimately for. I can begin practising the genre early — even a conceptual argument can be translated into a brief for a practitioner or commissioning audience — and the skill compounds as my evidence base grows. By the time I hold research findings of my own, translating them into decisions will already be second nature.

This output also sits at the intersection of all my strands: the researcher who generates the evidence, the learning designer who makes it accessible, and the public writer who makes it land. A strong policy brief is where those identities stop being separate and become one capability — evidence, made usable, aimed at the people who can close the gap.
