# 💼 Academic & Fellowship Job Application

> **A complete reference for the post-doctoral market — postdocs, fellowships, and academic posts**
> After the PhD, a distinct set of documents decides what comes next. The academic job application is its own genre, quite different from the graduate application that came before: I am no longer asking to be trained, I am presenting myself as an independent researcher with a programme, a teaching identity, and a contribution to make. This guide covers the research statement, the teaching statement, the cover letter, and the job talk.

## 1. What Has Changed Since the PhD Application

> **My reality:** the graduate application asked whether I could be trained. This one asks whether I am ready to work as an independent scholar. The whole register shifts.

Post-doctoral applications assess a track record and a trajectory, not potential. Search committees and fellowship panels are asking: does this person have a coherent research programme, evidence they can deliver it, and the makings of a colleague — as a researcher, a teacher, and a contributor to a scholarly community? My materials must present a scholar who is already producing, already has a direction, and already knows what they are building.

For my portfolio path, this phase is also where I decide how much of an academic career I want, alongside the clinical, consulting, and creative strands of my work. Not every strong researcher takes a conventional faculty post — and the fellowship route, in particular, can fund independent research without requiring one.

> 💡 **Key question:** Do my materials describe a researcher with a *programme* — a coherent line of inquiry with a past, present, and future — or a graduate with a completed thesis? The difference is what separates a competitive application from an also-ran.

## 2. The Cover Letter

The cover letter frames the entire application and is often read first. It makes the case for fit in a single, well-argued page or two.

- Open with who I am as a researcher and the core of my contribution — not with the job title I am applying for
- Show specific fit with *this* post and *this* department: the colleagues, the strengths, the reason we are a match
- Signal the shape of my research programme and my teaching, each in a paragraph, pointing to the fuller statements
- Close with why this role, at this moment, is the right next step

> ⚠️ **Avoid:** A generic letter with the institution's name swapped in. Committees read hundreds; the ones that get attention are visibly written for that specific department by someone who has understood what it is.

## 3. The Research Statement

The research statement is the heart of the application: a coherent account of my research past, present, and future.

---

### 3.1 What It Covers

- **The through-line** — the coherent question or problem my work addresses, stated so a non-specialist committee member grasps its significance
- **What I have done** — my key contributions, framed as a record, not a list: what the field knows because of my work
- **What I am doing now** — current projects and their trajectory
- **Where it is going** — the next five years: the programme I would build, the grants I would pursue, the questions I would open

> 💡 **Key question:** Could a committee, after reading my research statement, describe my programme back to me in a sentence — and see how the next five years follow from the last? Coherence and forward momentum are what they are reading for.

---

### 3.2 Making It Fundable and Feasible

- Show that my future programme is fundable — that it maps onto real funding streams (for me, CIHR, SSHRC, Wellcome)
- Demonstrate independence: that I can drive a research agenda, not only execute a supervisor's
- Where relevant, show how my programme fits and strengthens the hiring department's existing work

---

## 4. The Teaching Statement

For posts with a teaching component, the teaching statement demonstrates that I have thought seriously about education — which, given my learning-design background, is a genuine strength to foreground rather than a box to tick.

- **My philosophy** — what I believe about how people learn, grounded in more than sentiment
- **My practice** — concrete examples of how that philosophy shows up in how I teach
- **Inclusion and accessibility** — how I make learning reach all students; for me this is central expertise, not an add-on
- **Evidence** — teaching experience, evaluations, materials I have designed

> 📝 **Tip:** My learning-systems and accessibility work over the past decade make my teaching statement distinctive. Where many candidates write a philosophy they hope to grow into, I can write one I have already practised at scale. That is a genuine and rare advantage.

---

## 5. The Job Talk

Most academic posts and many fellowships require a presentation — the job talk — where I present my research to the department in person.

- Present a coherent research story, not a single paper: where I have been, what I found, where I am going
- Pitch to a mixed audience — specialists and non-specialists in the same room — and make the significance legible to both
- Show I would be a good colleague: how I think, how I handle questions, how I hold disagreement
- Handle the Q&A as the real test it is — reasoned, generous, and unflustered, exactly as the viva taught me

> 💡 **Key question:** Would the people in that room want me in the office next door — as a thinker, a collaborator, and a teacher? The job talk assesses collegiality as much as research, and both are on show.

## Writing Process & Strategy

### ✍️ Postdocs and Fellowships vs Faculty Posts

| Feature | Postdoc / fellowship | Faculty post |
|---------|---------------------|--------------|
| Emphasis | Research programme and its fundability | Research *and* teaching *and* service |
| Independence | Often developing, under a mentor | Fully independent expected |
| Teaching materials | Sometimes not required | Usually required |
| The pitch | A fundable project and its promise | A colleague for the long term |

- Fellowships can fund an independent research line without a conventional post — a strong fit for a portfolio career
- I tailor the balance of research and teaching emphasis to the specific role, never sending one generic set

---

### ✅ Application Checklist

- [ ] Every document is tailored to the specific role and department
- [ ] Cover letter argues specific fit, not generic enthusiasm
- [ ] Research statement presents a coherent programme with past, present, and future
- [ ] The through-line is legible to a non-specialist
- [ ] Future programme is shown to be fundable and independent
- [ ] Teaching statement pairs philosophy with concrete practice
- [ ] Accessibility and inclusion are addressed with real substance
- [ ] Job talk tells a research story, not a single paper
- [ ] Q&A is prepared for as a genuine assessment
- [ ] The materials present a colleague, not a graduate

---

### 🎓 Notes for My Pathway

This phase is where my unconventional profile becomes a decisive asset rather than a liability. A candidate who arrives with deep learning-design and accessibility expertise, a body of public scholarship, real-world practice, and a coherent research programme on accessible depression care is not a standard applicant — and standard is not what distinguishes a field.

It is also where I get to choose. The portfolio career I am building does not require a conventional faculty post; fellowships can fund independent research, and my clinical, consulting, and creative strands offer income and meaning that free me to be selective. These materials are how I pursue the academic strand on my own terms — as one part of a larger working life, not the whole of it.

- Write the research statement first. The cover letter and job talk are compressed versions of it.
- Keep one master CV and one master teaching dossier (see `COURSE_DESIGN_AND_TEACHING_DOSSIER.md`), and tailor them in under an hour per post.
- Turn the PhD's final chapter into the job talk, so you aren't writing it from scratch.
