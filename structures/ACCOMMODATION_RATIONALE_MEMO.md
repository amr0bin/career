# ⚖️ Accommodation Rationale & Decision Memo

> **The core written product of an Accessibility Advisor: a reasoned, defensible accommodation decision.**
> An advisor's real skill is adjudication — deciding, on an institution's behalf, what a student is entitled to and why — and then writing it so a student, an instructor, a manager and, if it comes to that, a tribunal can all follow the reasoning. This is the document that closes the gap between my record and the advisor posting.

---

## 1. The structure

| Part | What it does | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Request and context | What was asked for, by whom, for which course or programme, and when | Facts only; no diagnosis needed beyond functional impact |
| Functional impact | How the barrier shows up in this learning environment | Social model: describe the interaction of person and environment, not the condition |
| Essential requirements | What the course or programme must assess, stated as constructs | Separate the construct from the method of assessing it |
| Options considered | Each reasonable accommodation, with its effect on the essential requirement | Name at least two options |
| Undue hardship analysis | Whether any option would cause undue hardship, with evidence | Cost, health and safety, outside sources of funding — not inconvenience |
| Decision | What is approved, for how long, and how it will be reviewed | Interim measures if a full decision needs time |
| Rationale | Why, in plain language, tied to policy and law | Cite the institution's policy and the governing tests |
| Review and appeal | How the student can ask for reconsideration | Never a dead end |

---

## 2. How to do it well

1. Start from the essential requirement, not the accommodation: ask what the assessment is actually measuring.
2. Apply the three-step test from *Meiorin* to the requirement: rational connection, adopted in good faith, reasonably necessary (accommodation to the point of undue hardship).
3. Remember *Moore*: the duty applies to meaningful access to education, and the comparison is with the service as a whole, not with other disabled students.
4. Ask for documentation only to the extent needed to establish functional impact (the AHEAD documentation guidance and the OHRC policy both limit it).
5. Write the rationale so the student could read it without feeling assessed as a person: the decision is about the barrier.
6. Record the review date. Fluctuating conditions need decisions built to fluctuate.
7. Have a second advisor read every tenth memo as quality assurance.

## 🧭 Credible examples, fast path and where this sits now

### 📚 Credible examples

- *British Columbia (Public Service Employee Relations Commission) v. BCGSEU*, [1999] 3 SCR 3 (*Meiorin*) — the unified test for a bona fide requirement.
- *Moore v. British Columbia (Education)*, 2012 SCC 61 — the duty to accommodate in education.
- Ontario Human Rights Commission. (2018). *Policy on Accessible Education for Students with Disabilities*. The most detailed Canadian guidance on documentation, essential requirements and the accommodation process.
- AHEAD. (2012). *Supporting Accommodation Requests: Guidance on Documentation Practices*.
- BC Human Rights Tribunal accommodation decisions (published on bchrt.bc.ca): read one a month, as the standing rhythm already says.

### ⚡ Do it well and fast

- Keep a template with the eight parts; a well-practised memo takes 30–45 minutes.
- **House rule 5: only invented cases, clearly marked as invented.** Never a real case, even de-identified, to show the skill.
- Build a casebook of invented determinations (the 'living as the advisor' weekly half-page): one a week, one variable changed each time.

### ✅ Checklist

- [ ] The essential requirement is stated as a construct
- [ ] At least two options are weighed
- [ ] Undue hardship is argued with evidence, not asserted
- [ ] Documentation asked for is limited to functional impact
- [ ] A review date and an appeal route are given
- [ ] It is an invented case, labelled as such, if it leaves the office
