# 🎤 Conference Abstract & Presentation

> **A complete reference for getting my work accepted and presenting it well — from MSc through early career**

The presentation is the performance — a short window to make my work land with the people best placed to use, cite, or fund it. Both are learnable structures, and both begin earlier in my path than I might expect.

## 1. Why Conferences Matter to Me

> **My reality:** conference presentation is how I enter the field as a person, not just a name on a paper — and it starts as early as the MSc.

Conferences do things publication cannot. They put me in the room with the researchers whose work I build on, and let me be known to them before I ever apply to work with them. They surface feedback on work in progress while it can still change. And they build the visible track record — presented at X, invited to Y — that makes an unconventional academic profile credible. For someone deliberately building a research presence before holding senior credentials, presenting is one of the highest-return activities available.

> 💡 **Key question:** Who in the room do I most want to know my work — and does my abstract and talk speak to *them*? A conference is an audience I have chosen; I should pitch to the people I came to reach.

## 2. The Abstract

> **Typical length:** 200–300 words · submitted months ahead · often the only thing reviewers see

The abstract is a self-contained argument for why my work deserves a place on the programme. It follows the shape of the work itself.

| Element | What it does |
|---------|-------------|
| **Background / problem** | Why this matters, in one or two sentences |
| **Aim** | The specific question or objective |
| **Method** | How I investigated it — enough to judge rigour |
| **Results** | What I found (or, for work in progress, expected findings and current status) |
| **Conclusion / significance** | What it contributes and why the audience should care |

- Write to the conference's theme and to its review criteria — reviewers score against them
- Be concrete about findings; vague abstracts ("results will be discussed") are weaker than specific ones
- Respect the word limit exactly; over-length abstracts are often rejected unread

> ⚠️ **Avoid:** Promising more than I can deliver by the conference date. If data will still be in analysis, I present it honestly as work in progress — reviewers accept that, and disappointing an audience with unfinished promises is worse than pitching accurately.

## 3. Oral Presentations

A conference talk is usually short — often ten to fifteen minutes plus questions. The discipline is ruthless selection: one clear message, well delivered.

- **One takeaway:** the audience will remember a single thing; I decide in advance what it is and build the talk around it
- **Structure:** a brief hook, the question, the method in essentials, the key finding, and what it means — in that order
- **Slides carry images and data, not paragraphs:** the slide supports what I say; it is not a script for the audience to read
- **Timing is respected absolutely:** running over is read as a discourtesy to the speakers after me, and chairs will cut me off

> 📝 **Tip:** I rehearse aloud, to time, more than once. The gap between knowing my work and delivering it clearly in twelve minutes is larger than it looks, and it closes only with spoken practice.

## 4. Poster Presentations

Posters are common for earlier-stage work and are a genuine skill of their own — visual communication under severe space constraints.

- One clear visual argument, readable from a distance, with generous white space
- Minimal text: the poster orients; the conversation carries the detail
- A short spoken summary ready for anyone who stops — thirty seconds that make them want the longer version
- Contact details and a way to follow the work, so a good conversation does not end at the poster

> 💡 **Key question:** From two metres away, can a passer-by tell what my poster is about and why it matters? If the main message is not instantly legible, the design is working against me.

---

## 5. Handling Questions

The question period builds — or dents — reputation more than the talk itself. It is where the field sees how I think on my feet.

- Listen to the whole question before answering; it is fine to pause and think
- Answer the question actually asked, briefly, rather than retreating to a rehearsed point
- When I do not know, I say so and reason aloud — that reads as maturity, not weakness
- Thank challenging questioners genuinely; a sharp question is often the most useful feedback I will get

> 📝 **Tip:** The questions I fear are the ones worth preparing. Before presenting, I list the three hardest things someone could ask and work out my answers — the same discipline the viva will later demand.

## Writing Process & Strategy

### ✍️ Recommended Order

| Order | Step | Why |
|-------|------|-----|
| 1 | **Choose the conference and read its call** | Fit and criteria shape the abstract |
| 2 | **Draft the abstract to the criteria** | It is written to be scored, in the reviewers' terms |
| 3 | **On acceptance, decide the one takeaway** | Everything in the talk or poster serves it |
| 4 | **Build slides or poster around the message** | Visual-first, minimal text |
| 5 | **Rehearse aloud, to time** | The only way delivery actually improves |
| 6 | **Prepare for the hardest questions** | Reputation is built in the Q&A |

---

### ✅ Submission & Presentation Checklist

- [ ] Abstract is written to the conference theme and review criteria
- [ ] Abstract has background, aim, method, results, and significance
- [ ] Findings are concrete; work-in-progress is labelled honestly
- [ ] Word limit is respected exactly
- [ ] The talk or poster has one clear takeaway
- [ ] Slides / poster are visual-first, with minimal text
- [ ] The presentation is rehearsed aloud and fits the time
- [ ] The three hardest likely questions are prepared for
- [ ] A way to follow up (contact, link) is provided
- [ ] The people I most want to reach are who the work is pitched to

### 🎓 Notes for My Pathway

I do not wait for the PhD to present. A well-received MSc systematic review is legitimate conference material, and presenting it early puts me in front of the exact community — arts and health, global mental health, implementation science — I am trying to join. Each presentation also rehearses, at low stakes, the spoken defence of my work that confirmation and the viva will later demand at high stakes.

Conference presenting and my public scholarship do related work at different registers: one reaches the field, the other reaches practitioners and the public. Together they build the visible presence that makes a non-traditional researcher known before the credentials are complete.
