# 🛡️ Research Ethics Application

> **A complete reference for the approval I must secure before any data collection — MSc, MA, and PhD**
> An ethics application is the formal document that authorises me to involve human participants in research. No data may be collected until it is approved. It is not bureaucratic box-ticking: it is where I demonstrate that I have thought carefully about my responsibility to the people my research touches — which, for work on depression, matters more than almost anywhere.

## 1. What the Committee Is Protecting

> **My reality:** ethics approval sits on the critical path. Data collection cannot begin until it is granted, and review takes weeks. It is planned into the timeline from the start, never left to the end.

A research ethics committee (REC / IRB) exists to protect participants, not to obstruct me. Every question on the form is a version of one underlying question: *have I minimised the risk to the people involved, and can they make a genuinely free and informed choice about taking part?* When I read the form that way, the answers write themselves from the participant's side of the table.

For research on depression the committee's attention is heightened, and rightly so. My participants may be distressed, may be vulnerable, and may find the research process itself difficult. A strong application shows that I have anticipated this and built protection in — not that I have promised nothing will ever go wrong.

> 💡 **Key question:** If I were the participant — or their advocate — would I feel respected, protected, and free to say no or to stop? If any part of my design would not survive that test, it needs to change before I submit.

## 2. The Core Components

Committees use their own forms, but almost all require the same substance. I prepare these regardless of the specific template.

- **Study summary** — the research question, design, and what participation involves, in plain language
- **Recruitment** — who I will approach, how, and why that method is not coercive
- **Informed consent process** — how participants are informed and how consent is obtained and recorded
- **Risk assessment** — risks to participants and to me, and how each is mitigated
- **Data management** — how data are collected, stored, anonymised, and eventually destroyed
- **Confidentiality and its limits** — how identity is protected, and the specific circumstances in which confidentiality would have to be broken
- **Supporting documents** — participant information sheet, consent form, recruitment materials, interview schedule or instruments, distress protocol

> 📝 **Tip:** The committee reads the participant-facing documents as closely as the form itself. A carelessly written information sheet undermines an otherwise strong application, because it is the part the participant actually experiences.

## 3. Consent and Information

Informed consent is the ethical heart of the application. It rests on two documents that must be genuinely usable by the people receiving them.

---

### 3.1 The Participant Information Sheet

Written in plain language, at an accessible reading level, it tells the participant everything they need to decide freely:

- What the study is about and why it is being done
- What taking part actually involves — time, activities, what is asked of them
- The risks and any benefits, described honestly
- That participation is voluntary and can be withdrawn without penalty
- What happens to their data, and how their identity is protected
- Who to contact, including an independent route for concerns

> ⚠️ **Avoid:** Information sheets written in academic register. If a distressed participant cannot easily understand what they are agreeing to, consent is not informed — no matter what they sign. Accessibility here is an ethical requirement, and one I take personally seriously.

---

### 3.2 The Consent Form

A short document recording that the participant has understood and agreed. It typically confirms, item by item, that they have read the information, understood that participation is voluntary, know they can withdraw, and agree to how their data will be used.

- Match every claim on the consent form to something described in the information sheet
- Handle recordings, quotation, and data reuse as separate, explicit consents where relevant
- Address the right to withdraw concretely: by when, how, and what happens to already-collected data

## 4. Risk and Vulnerability

This is the section that matters most for my field, and the one a committee will scrutinise hardest.

---

### 4.1 Anticipating Distress

Research on depression can surface distress simply by asking about it. I plan for that.

- A **distress protocol**: what I will do, step by step, if a participant becomes distressed during data collection — pause, check in, offer to stop, signpost support
- A **signposting list**: relevant support services provided to every participant, regardless of whether distress occurs
- Clear boundaries: I am a researcher in this encounter, not their therapist, and the protocol keeps that line safe for both of us

> 💡 **Key question:** If a participant discloses that they are at risk during a session, do I know exactly what I will do — and have I told them, in advance, the limits of confidentiality? The answer must be worked out before I submit, not improvised in the room.

---

### 4.2 Confidentiality and Its Limits

I cannot promise absolute confidentiality, and pretending otherwise is itself unethical.

- State plainly, in the information sheet, the specific circumstances in which I would have to share information (serious risk of harm to self or others)
- Explain what that sharing would involve, so consent genuinely accounts for it
- Distinguish confidentiality (I protect their identity) from anonymity (their identity is not known at all) accurately

---

### 4.3 Power and Positionality

Committees increasingly expect me to reflect on the power dynamics in my research.

- Am I recruiting from a population I have a prior relationship with, or authority over?
- Could participants feel implicit pressure to take part or to give particular answers?
- For any international or cross-cultural work, does the design respect the community rather than extract from it?

## 5. Data Protection

Committees expect a concrete, compliant plan for the whole data lifecycle — not a vague assurance that data will be "kept safe."

- **Collection:** exactly what personal and special-category data I gather (health data is special-category and attracts stricter rules)
- **Storage:** where, encrypted, access-controlled, and separated from identifying information
- **Anonymisation / pseudonymisation:** how and when identifiers are removed or replaced
- **Retention and destruction:** how long data are kept, and how they are destroyed
- **Sharing:** who else, if anyone, has access, and under what agreement

> 📝 **Tip:** I align this section with the data-management plan in any parallel grant or proposal. Committees notice when the ethics application and the funded plan describe different things.

## Writing Process & Strategy

### ✍️ Recommended Order

| Order | Step | Why |
|-------|------|-----|
| 1 | **Finalise the method first** | I cannot describe risks and data flows for a design that is still moving |
| 2 | **Draft the information sheet and consent form** | Writing them surfaces the ethical issues the form then asks about |
| 3 | **Write the distress and risk protocols** | The core of the case for research on depression |
| 4 | **Complete the data-management section** | Concrete, compliant, and matched to any grant plan |
| 5 | **Fill the committee form last** | It becomes a summary of decisions already made |
| 6 | **Have my supervisor review before submission** | Supervisors know what this committee, specifically, looks for |

### ✍️ Why Applications Get Returned

Most first submissions come back with revisions. The common reasons are avoidable:

- An information sheet that is too complex for the intended participants
- No distress protocol, or a vague one, for sensitive research
- Confidentiality promised without stating its limits
- A data plan that names no retention period or destruction method
- A recruitment route that a committee reads as coercive

Treating a return as normal — and responding point by point — is how approval is reached without drama.

---

### ✅ Submission Checklist

- [ ] Method is finalised before the application is written
- [ ] Study summary is in plain language
- [ ] Recruitment route is non-coercive and justified
- [ ] Information sheet is genuinely accessible to the intended participants
- [ ] Consent form matches every claim in the information sheet
- [ ] Voluntary participation and right to withdraw are concrete
- [ ] A distress protocol is in place for sensitive content
- [ ] A signposting list is provided to all participants
- [ ] Confidentiality limits are stated honestly
- [ ] Power and positionality are addressed
- [ ] Data storage, anonymisation, retention, and destruction are specified
- [ ] Special-category (health) data handling is compliant
- [ ] Supervisor has reviewed before submission
- [ ] Ethics timeline is built into the overall research plan

### 🎓 Notes for My Pathway

Ethics approval recurs at every research stage — the MSc systematic review may need only light review, but any primary data at MA or PhD level requires a full application. Because approval sits on the critical path and takes weeks, I treat it as a fixed early milestone in every project timeline, not a formality to clear at the end.

For my subject specifically, the ethics application is also where my values become visible. Accessibility and care for vulnerable participants are the substance of my research programme, so a thoughtful, humane application is not a hoop — it is a direct expression of the work.
