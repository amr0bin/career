# How to write a thesis

> **Purpose:** the craft of writing a thesis, in eight competencies, each applied to my own thesis line. `arc/the-thesis-line.md` says *what* the thesis is; this file says *how* to write it. · **Governs:** how every piece of the thesis line is written, from the Leeds module essays to the PhD dissertation. · **Carried out in Notion:** [How to write a thesis](https://app.notion.com/p/40cd55c9671b81828b9fcd027273a298) (the checklist page), [Tasks](https://app.notion.com/p/1dca49d1884242e28a2f2d21f1216be9), [Learning](https://app.notion.com/p/ffc3561528954f98ad4562b1c5e48b93)

## The ceiling

A thesis written this way is not one document produced once. It is a method practised nine times as Leeds essays, once as a supervised pilot, once as an MA thesis and once as a dissertation, so that by 2031 the MA is the fourth time I have done each step, not the first. At full height the MA thesis is defended on schedule, splits into two journal papers (the Leeds framing and the UBC framing named in the thesis line), and hands the PhD a tested design. The eight competencies below are what make that repeatable.

**Guides for every other kind of document** (papers, proposals, ethics, clinical, advisor and teaching writing): [`../structures/README.md`](../structures/README.md).

## The short version

Do these in order. Each one depends on the one before it.

1. **Name the gap.** Say exactly where the existing map of knowledge ends.
2. **Turn the topic into a question.** One sentence that could be answered, with a population, a process and an outcome.
3. **Pick the reporting guideline.** It tells you what every chapter must contain before you write a word.
4. **Build the skeleton.** Chapters, then headings, then a word budget for each heading.
5. **Set up citations on day one.** One reference manager, one style, every source entered when it is read.
6. **Choose the kind of literature review on purpose.** Know which of the four you are doing and why.
7. **Write to the skeleton, a heading at a time.** Half a page about one subheading is a full writing session.
8. **Lead the supervisor relationship.** Bring an agenda, a draft and a specific question to every meeting.

## 1 · Find the gap

**The rule:** a gap is not unexplored territory. It is the edge of a map that already exists. You find it by reading the map, not by looking for empty space.

**The common kinds of gap:** evidence (findings disagree), knowledge (something has not been studied), practical (what practitioners know has not been studied), methodological (it has only been studied one way), empirical (a claim has not been tested), theoretical (no theory explains it, or the wrong one is being used), and population (it has not been studied in this group or place). One clear gap is enough. Two named weakly are worse than one named well.

**What to do:** write the gap as one sentence that starts "We know … but we do not know …". Cite three to five sources for the first half.

**In my thesis:** we know disabled students report lower wellbeing and that accommodation processes are slow and documentation-heavy. We do not know whether the *process itself* acts as a disabling barrier, or which of its design features carry the effect. That is a theoretical gap (the student-deficit framing is the wrong theory; psycho-emotional disablism is the better one) and an empirical gap (the link has not been tested at the level of process features).

**Practised first:** *Disability and Inequality* (Leeds Year 1), written as Literature Review I.

## 2 · Build the research question

**The rule:** a topic is a direction; a question is a destination. If you cannot say what an answer would look like, you have a topic.

**What to do:** check the question against four tests. *Answerable* (data could settle it). *Specific* (names the population, the process or exposure, and the outcome). *Bounded* (one study can hold it). *Worth it* (the answer would change something someone controls).

**In my thesis:** *Does the university accommodation process act as a disabling barrier in its own right, and through which of its design features does it shape disabled students' wellbeing and help-seeking?* Population: disabled students who have used, abandoned or decided against the process. Exposure: process features (documentation asked for, waits, re-proving, fit with fluctuation). Outcomes: wellbeing, depressive symptoms, help-seeking. Who controls the answer: the university. It passes all four tests.

**Practised first:** the four-literature paper (now), then *Doing Disability Research* (Leeds Year 1).

## 3 · Use a reporting guideline

**The rule:** every type of study has a published checklist of what a write-up must report. It is the thesis's blueprint. Writing without one means discovering missing sections at the examination.

**What to do:** pick the guideline that matches the design, copy its items into the skeleton as placeholder headings, and tick each item off as it is written.

**In my thesis line:**

| Piece | Design | Guideline |
|---|---|---|
| Scoping review | Scoping review | PRISMA-ScR |
| Corpus study | Document analysis | SRQR (qualitative) for the reporting frame |
| Leeds pilot and MA thesis | Mixed methods: survey plus interviews | GRAMMS for the mixed-methods whole; STROBE for the cross-sectional survey strand; SRQR or COREQ for the interview strand |
| PhD intervention study | Feasibility, then hybrid effectiveness-implementation | CONSORT extension for pilot and feasibility trials; StaRI for the implementation strand |

All of these are free at the EQUATOR Network (equator-network.org).

**Practised first:** the scoping review protocol (PRISMA-ScR), now.

## 4 · Structure, format and weight every chapter

**The rule:** the reason a thesis is hard to start is almost never the writing. It is that the structure does not exist yet. A thesis is an engineered document: a fixed sequence of chapters, each with a known job and a known share of the length.

**What to do:** build the skeleton before drafting: chapters, then the headings inside each, then a word budget per heading. A typical empirical thesis weights roughly like this (confirm against the programme's own rules):

| Chapter | Its one job | Rough share |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | The problem, the gap, the question, why it matters | ~10% |
| Literature review | The map, and where it ends | ~20–25% |
| Methods | What was done, so it could be repeated or judged | ~15–20% |
| Results | What was found, without interpretation | ~20–25% |
| Discussion | What it means, against the literature; limits | ~15–20% |
| Conclusion | The answer, and what changes because of it | ~5% |

**In my thesis:** the Leeds module map in `arc/the-thesis-line.md` *is* this skeleton. Understanding Disability is the theoretical frame; the two literature modules are the review; the rights and policy modules are the legal and policy frame; Doing Disability Research is the methods chapter; the Applied Project supplies results; Building Enabling Futures seeds the discussion. Each essay is written to drop into its chapter.

**Practised first:** set up the skeleton for the four-literature paper (under 6,000 words) with a budget per section before drafting.

## 5 · Cite correctly and easily

**The rule:** most plagiarism is accidental, and almost all of it comes from notes that lost their source. Citation is a system set up on day one, not a repair job at the end.

**What to do:**

- One reference manager for everything (Zotero is free and works with Word and Google Docs).
- One style: **APA 7**, the standard in counselling psychology and education.
- Enter the source when it is read, not when it is cited.
- In notes, keep the source's words and my words visibly separate (quotation marks plus page number for theirs; plain text for mine).
- File each reading note in the Second Brain against the favourite problem it serves, with its citation key, so notes are already citable when drafting starts.

**In my thesis:** the corpus study's capture rule (save a PDF and an Internet Archive link the same day) is the same habit applied to documents. Apply it to every source.

**Practised first:** now, with the four-literature paper's reading.

## 6 · Do the right kind of literature review

**The rule:** "do a literature review" can mean four different things. Choosing the wrong one costs weeks.

| Kind | What it is | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Narrative (background) | An argued overview of the field | The literature chapter of most theses; the Leeds essays |
| Systematic | A protocol-driven search answering a narrow question, with appraisal | When the question is narrow and the evidence base is large |
| Scoping | A protocol-driven map of what exists on a broad question, without appraisal | When the field is young or scattered (my scoping review) |
| Integrative or critical | Brings different kinds of evidence or theory together to build a new frame | When the contribution is the framework itself (my four-literature paper) |

**What to do:** name the kind in the first paragraph of the review and follow its method.

**In my thesis:** the MA's literature chapter is narrative, built from the Leeds essays, and it cites the scoping review and the four-literature paper as my own prior work. Three kinds, each doing a different job, all feeding one chapter.

## 7 · Build a writing routine

**The rule:** thesis writers rarely have a motivation problem. They have a structure problem. You never write a thesis; you write half a page about one subheading.

**What to do:**

- Each session has one target: one heading from the skeleton.
- Write in the protected block already set in This Week; the skeleton decides what goes in it.
- Draft first, cite with placeholder keys, polish later. Do not edit and draft in the same session.
- End each session by writing the next session's first sentence.
- Log words or headings done in the Weekly Log, so progress is visible across months.

**In my thesis line:** during Leeds, each module essay is the routine's unit. In module breaks, the routine moves to the scoping review and the corpus study.

## 8 · Work with the supervisor

**The rule:** a supervisor does not tell you what to do next. You lead the process; the supervisor responds to it.

**What to do before every meeting:** send, two working days ahead, a one-paragraph update, the draft or section in question, and two or three specific questions ("Is the process-feature list complete?", not "What do you think?").

**After every meeting:** send a short email listing what was agreed and the next deadline. File it in the Supervision Log.

**At the start:** agree expectations in writing: meeting frequency, turnaround time on drafts, how feedback is given, authorship on papers from the thesis.

**In my thesis line:** the sequence in `arc/the-thesis-line.md` (the 2029 report and one-page fit note, the 2030 design conversation, the autumn 2030 draft proposal) is this competency applied before the MA begins. The supervisor meets a researcher who already leads.

## Support that multiplies

- **A writing group or thesis buddy.** Weekly accountability doubles output for most writers. Leeds and UBC graduate schools both run writing groups and retreats; join in the first term.
- **The library.** Subject librarians help build search strategies for systematic and scoping reviews; book one before the scoping review search is run.
- **The graduate writing centre.** UBC's Centre for Writing and Scholarly Communication offers thesis consultations.
- **The co-researcher and advisory group** read chapters as they are written, not only findings.
- **A second reader** outside the field, once per chapter, to catch what the supervisor is too close to see.

**Fallback, once:** if a chapter stalls, drop to the heading level and write the smallest piece in the skeleton. The structure carries the work forward.
