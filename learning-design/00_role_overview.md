# 00 · Role Overview: Learning Experience Designer

## What This Role Is

A Learning Experience Designer (LXD) — also called Learning Designer or Learning Design Specialist — partners with subject matter experts (SMEs), faculty, and institutional stakeholders to design and develop high-quality, learner-centred educational experiences.

The role sits at the intersection of **pedagogy, project management, technology, and communication**. Unlike a traditional instructional designer who may work more independently on content, an LXD functions as a **consultant and collaborator** — guiding decision-making, recommending strategies, and co-creating solutions with the people who know the content.

---

## Core Responsibilities

| Area | What It Looks Like in Practice |
|---|---|
| **Course Design** | Leading new course development or redesign from intake to launch |
| **Assessment Design** | Recommending diverse, outcomes-aligned assessments; supporting academic integrity by design |
| **Instructional Strategy** | Advising on engagement, motivation, and active learning approaches |
| **Content Development** | Collaborating on learning materials, media, and course site structure |
| **Technology Integration** | Supporting faculty in adopting and embedding tools into teaching |
| **Accessibility** | Ensuring materials meet accessibility standards; embedding UDL principles |
| **Evaluation & Refinement** | Using analytics, feedback, and evaluations to recommend ongoing improvements |
| **Project Management** | Managing timelines, stakeholders, and scope across the full design lifecycle |

---

## The Consultant Mindset

This role is fundamentally **advisory and relational**. You are not just producing deliverables — you are building trust, managing expectations, and helping SMEs articulate what they want and need (which are often different things).

Key mindset shifts for success:

- **Ask before assuming.** A needs assessment conversation is always worth the time.
- **Surface the real goal.** SMEs often arrive with a solution ("I want a video"). Your job is to uncover the learning problem underneath.
- **Protect the learner's perspective.** You are the learner's advocate in every design conversation.
- **Be a translator.** Between pedagogy and practice, between SME expertise and learner context, between institutional goals and course realities.
- **Hold the process.** Even when stakeholders want to skip steps, keep the project grounded in sound design principles.

## Core Competencies

### Pedagogical
- Learning theories (behaviourism, constructivism, connectivism)
- Instructional design models (ADDIE, SAM, Agile/iterative)
- Curriculum and assessment design
- Universal Design for Learning (UDL)
- Active learning and engagement strategies

### Technical
- LMS administration and course design (Canvas, Moodle, Brightspace, etc.)
- Authoring tools (Articulate Storyline/Rise, H5P, Adobe Captivate)
- Media production basics (audio, video, graphic design)
- Accessibility standards (WCAG 2.2; in Canada the *Accessible Canada Act*, the *Accessible British Columbia Act*, Ontario's AODA, and CAN/ASC-EN 301 549 for ICT procurement; Section 508 for US clients)
- Project management tools (Jira, Trello, Asana, Microsoft Planner)

### Interpersonal
- Stakeholder management and consultation
- Facilitation and workshop design
- Written, verbal, and visual communication
- Conflict navigation and scope management
- Feedback and coaching

---

## How This Role Fits in a Team

In most institutional learning design units, LXDs work within a team that may also include:

- **Learning Technologists** — focus on tools, platforms, and technical implementation
- **Media Producers / Multimedia Specialists** — handle video, audio, and graphic assets
- **Project Managers or Coordinators** — manage timelines and resource allocation
- **Accessibility Specialists** — ensure materials meet standards
- **Evaluators / Researchers** — assess program effectiveness

As an LXD, you often act as the **project lead and primary point of contact** for the faculty/SME, coordinating inputs from these other specialists as needed.

---

## Measures of Success

- Courses launch on time and to scope
- Faculty feel supported and confident post-engagement
- Learners can navigate, engage with, and succeed in the course
- Assessment and learning outcomes are meaningfully aligned
- The design is sustainable — faculty can maintain and update it independently
- You leave behind transferable skills and documentation, not just a finished course
