# 06 · Learning Outcomes and Alignment

## What Learning Outcomes Are (and Aren't)

A **learning outcome** is a statement of what learners will be able to *do* as a result of instruction — not what they will be *exposed to* or what the instructor will *cover*.

| ❌ Not an outcome | ✅ Is an outcome |
|---|---|
| "Students will learn about project management" | "Students will develop a project plan using standard PM frameworks" |
| "This module covers communication theory" | "Students will apply communication theory to analyse a real workplace conflict" |
| "Understanding climate change" | "Students will evaluate the trade-offs of three climate policy approaches" |

**Outcomes answer the question:** *"What will a learner be able to do with what they've learned, in what context, and to what standard?"*

---

## The ABCD Formula for Writing Outcomes

A useful structure for writing clear, assessable outcomes:

| Letter | Element | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Audience | Who is doing the learning? (Usually "Students will..." or "Learners will...") |
| **B** | Behaviour | The observable, measurable action (a Bloom's verb) |
| **C** | Condition | Under what circumstances will they perform? |
| **D** | Degree | To what standard or level of proficiency? |

**Example:**
> *Students will [A] analyse [B] a given business case [C] and identify at least three strategic options with supporting rationale [D].*

Not every outcome needs all four elements — but the **Behaviour** component (the verb) is always non-negotiable.

## Bloom's Taxonomy: Choosing the Right Verb

Bloom's Taxonomy (revised by Anderson & Krathwohl) describes six levels of cognitive complexity. The verb you choose signals the level of thinking you expect.

### Cognitive Domain (Revised Bloom's)

| Level | Description | Strong Verbs |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Remember** | Recall facts and basic concepts | define, list, recall, identify, name, recognise, reproduce |
| **2. Understand** | Explain ideas or concepts | explain, describe, summarise, classify, paraphrase, interpret |
| **3. Apply** | Use information in new situations | apply, demonstrate, use, solve, implement, execute, carry out |
| **4. Analyse** | Draw connections; break into parts | analyse, compare, differentiate, examine, distinguish, organise |
| **5. Evaluate** | Justify a decision or course of action | evaluate, assess, critique, judge, justify, argue, defend |
| **6. Create** | Produce new or original work | design, construct, develop, produce, compose, plan, formulate |

### Affective Domain (Attitudes and Values)
For outcomes related to professional values, ethics, or dispositions:
- Receiving, responding, valuing, organising, characterising

### Psychomotor Domain (Skills and Performance)
For outcomes related to physical or procedural skills:
- Imitate, practise, perform, demonstrate, execute with precision

## Levels of Learning Outcomes

In most courses, outcomes exist at multiple levels. It's important to keep these aligned.

| Level | Scope | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Program outcomes** | What graduates of the full program can do | "Apply ethical reasoning to professional decisions in [field]" |
| **Course outcomes** | What learners can do after completing the course | "Evaluate ethical dilemmas using multiple ethical frameworks" |
| **Module/unit outcomes** | What learners can do after one module | "Explain utilitarianism and deontological ethics and contrast their approaches" |
| **Lesson outcomes** | What learners can do after a single lesson | "Define and give an example of a utilitarian ethical argument" |

**Rule:** Module and lesson outcomes should map clearly upward to course outcomes. Course outcomes should contribute to program outcomes.

## Constructive Alignment

Constructive alignment (Biggs & Tang) is the principle that outcomes, teaching/learning activities, and assessments should all be pointing at the same target.

### The Alignment Triangle

```
Learning Outcomes
      ↕
Teaching & Learning Activities ←→ Assessment Tasks
```

All three must be coherent. Misalignment is one of the most common and most damaging design problems in course development.

### Common Misalignments to Watch For

| Problem | Example |
|---|---|
| Outcome too low for assessment | Outcome: "Define key terms." Assessment: "Write a critical essay." |
| Outcome too high for instruction | Outcome: "Synthesise research." Activities: Read two articles; no synthesis practice. |
| Assessment measures something the outcome doesn't | Outcome: "Apply framework X." Assessment: "Write a reflection on your learning experience." |
| Activities that don't prepare learners for assessment | Assessment requires case analysis; no case practice in the course. |

## Writing Outcomes Collaboratively with SMEs

SMEs often describe their courses in terms of topics and content ("I cover X, Y, Z"). Your job is to translate that into outcomes.

**Useful prompts to elicit outcomes from SMEs:**
- "When a student leaves your course and goes into [career/next course/real life], what do you most want them to be able to do?"
- "If a student did really well in your course, what would they be able to do that a student who did poorly couldn't?"
- "What are the 3–5 most important things a learner will be able to do after completing this course?"
- "What would you look for in a strong final assignment?"

**From topic to outcome — a worked example:**

> SME says: *"I want to cover project management methodologies."*
>
> LXD asks: *"What should students be able to do with that knowledge when they leave?"*
>
> SME says: *"They should be able to choose the right methodology for a project."*
>
> Outcome: *"Students will select and justify an appropriate project management methodology for a given project context."* ✅

## Outcome Quality Checklist

Before finalising outcomes, check each one:

- [ ] Starts with an observable, measurable verb (not "understand," "appreciate," "be aware of")
- [ ] Specifies what the learner will do, not what the instructor will teach
- [ ] Is achievable within the scope of the course
- [ ] Is assessable — you can imagine evidence of its achievement
- [ ] Is aligned with at least one assessment task
- [ ] Is written in learner-friendly language (can be shared directly with students)
- [ ] Is distinct from other outcomes in the course (no overlaps or redundancies)

---

## Problematic Verbs to Avoid

These verbs are common but not measurable — they describe internal states, not observable behaviours:

- understand, know, appreciate, be familiar with, be aware of, learn about, explore, grasp, realise, recognise the importance of

**Replace with:** a verb that describes what learners will do to *demonstrate* that understanding.
