# 04 · Instructional Design Models

## Overview

Instructional design models provide structured frameworks for thinking about how to create effective learning experiences. No single model is right for every situation — experienced LXDs draw on multiple models depending on the project context, constraints, and goals.

> A model is a map, not the territory. Use it to orient yourself, not to constrain your thinking.

---

## The Major Models

### ADDIE
**Type:** Linear / Waterfall
**Best for:** Stable, well-defined projects; compliance training; projects requiring rigorous documentation

ADDIE stands for: **Analysis → Design → Development → Implementation → Evaluation**

| Phase | Key Questions | Typical Outputs |
|---|---|---|
| **Analysis** | Who are the learners? What's the gap? What are the constraints? | Needs assessment, learner profiles, gap analysis |
| **Design** | What will be learned? In what sequence? How assessed? | Learning outcomes, assessment strategy, course blueprint |
| **Development** | How will the content be built? What media and activities? | Storyboards, draft content, media assets |
| **Implementation** | How will the course be delivered? Is everything working? | LMS setup, faculty prep, launch |
| **Evaluation** | Did it work? What should change? | Formative and summative evaluation data |

**Strengths:** Comprehensive; documentation-heavy (good for regulated contexts); clear phase boundaries
**Limitations:** Slow; late stakeholder feedback; changes are costly once development begins; assumes requirements are stable

---

### SAM (Successive Approximation Model)
**Type:** Iterative / Agile-adjacent
**Best for:** Most higher ed and corporate learning design projects; when stakeholder feedback mid-process is valuable
**Developed by:** Allen Interactions (Michael Allen)

SAM exists in two versions:

#### SAM1 — For Smaller Projects
A rapid, 3-step cycle repeated until the product is ready:
1. **Evaluate** existing information and goals
2. **Design** a prototype (quick, rough, testable)
3. **Develop** based on feedback; repeat

#### SAM2 — For Larger Projects
Three phases, each iterative:

**Phase 1: Preparation**
- Information gathering (similar to ADDIE Analysis)
- Savvy Start workshop — rapid collaborative session to generate initial design ideas with SME and stakeholders

**Phase 2: Iterative Design**
- Design prototypes → get feedback → refine → repeat
- Produce: design proof (concept), alpha prototype (draft content)

**Phase 3: Iterative Development**
- Build and test in cycles: Alpha → Beta → Gold
- Each version is reviewed, tested, and refined before the next

**Strengths:** Faster stakeholder feedback; problems surface earlier; collaborative; flexible
**Limitations:** Requires disciplined facilitation; SME must commit to multiple feedback cycles; less documentation than ADDIE

---

### Agile Instructional Design
**Type:** Iterative / Sprint-based
**Best for:** Complex projects with evolving requirements; teams comfortable with self-organisation; digital-first courses
**See:** `03_agile_sprint_process.md` for full detail

Core difference from SAM: Agile is a **philosophy and set of principles**, not a prescriptive model. It can be implemented via Scrum, Kanban, or other frameworks.

---

### Backward Design (Understanding by Design — UbD)
**Type:** Outcomes-first / Conceptual
**Developed by:** Wiggins & McTighe
**Best for:** Curriculum planning; redesigns where alignment is poor; when starting from scratch with a clear purpose

Three stages — worked **in reverse order** from traditional design:

1. **Identify desired results** — What should learners know, understand, and be able to do?
2. **Determine acceptable evidence** — How will you know learners have achieved those results? (Design assessments first)
3. **Plan learning experiences** — What activities, content, and instruction will get learners there?

> UbD is not a project management model — it's a *design thinking* framework. Combine it with ADDIE or SAM for implementation.

**Strengths:** Forces alignment between outcomes, assessment, and instruction; prevents "activity-first" design
**Limitations:** Requires significant upfront clarity on goals; can feel abstract without a concrete implementation framework alongside it

---

### Gagne's Nine Events of Instruction
**Type:** Lesson-level design framework
**Best for:** Designing individual lessons, modules, or learning sequences
**Developed by:** Robert Gagné

| Event | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Gain attention | Engage the learner | Provocative question, case study, video hook |
| 2. Inform learner of objectives | Set expectations | "By the end of this module, you will be able to..." |
| 3. Stimulate recall of prior learning | Connect to existing knowledge | "Think back to what we covered in Unit 1..." |
| 4. Present content | Deliver new information | Lecture, reading, demonstration |
| 5. Provide learning guidance | Support processing | Examples, analogies, worked problems |
| 6. Elicit performance | Practice | Activity, quiz, discussion prompt |
| 7. Provide feedback | Inform and correct | Automated feedback, peer review, instructor response |
| 8. Assess performance | Measure learning | Graded assignment, quiz, demonstration |
| 9. Enhance retention and transfer | Help learners generalise | Application scenario, reflection prompt |

**Strengths:** Excellent for structuring individual lessons; grounded in cognitive learning theory
**Limitations:** Can feel prescriptive; not all nine events are needed in every lesson

## Comparison at a Glance

| Model | Scope | Approach | Stakeholder Involvement | Documentation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ADDIE | Full project | Linear | At phase gates | High |
| SAM | Full project | Iterative | Frequent (design cycles) | Medium |
| Agile/Scrum | Full project | Sprint-based | Continuous | Low–Medium |
| Backward Design | Curriculum/course | Conceptual framework | At planning stage | Low |
| Gagné's 9 Events | Lesson/module | Lesson structure | N/A | Low |

## Choosing a Model

Ask these questions:

- **How stable are the requirements?** → Stable: ADDIE works. Evolving: SAM or Agile.
- **How much time do we have?** → Limited: SAM1 or Agile sprints. Ample: ADDIE or SAM2.
- **How available is the SME?** → High availability: Agile or SAM (frequent collaboration). Low availability: ADDIE (front-load analysis; reduce check-in frequency).
- **Is this a new course or a redesign?** → New: Backward Design for goal-setting, then SAM or Agile for execution. Redesign: Start with gap analysis; use Agile or SAM for targeted improvement.
- **Is compliance or accreditation involved?** → Yes: ADDIE's documentation rigour is an asset.

---

## In Practice: Combining Models

Most experienced LXDs don't pick one model and follow it rigidly. A common combination:

1. Use **Backward Design** to establish outcomes and assessment strategy
2. Use **SAM or Agile** to manage the development process iteratively
3. Use **Gagné's 9 Events** to structure individual modules and lessons
