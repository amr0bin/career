# 05 · Assessment Design

## Why Assessment Design Matters

Assessment is not an afterthought — it is the clearest signal of what a course actually values. Learners study what is assessed. When assessments are poorly designed or misaligned with stated outcomes, learners learn the wrong things, or the right things in the wrong way.

As an LXD, your role in assessment design is to:
- Advocate for diverse, authentic, and aligned assessment strategies
- Help SMEs move beyond their default assessment habits
- Ensure assessments serve learning, not just grading
- Build in academic integrity by design

> Start with the end in mind. Design assessments before designing content. (See Backward Design in `04_instructional_design_models.md`)

## Formative vs. Summative Assessment

| Type | Purpose | Examples | Graded? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Formative** | Support learning in progress; provide feedback | Quizzes, discussion posts, drafts, polls, self-assessments | Usually low-stakes or ungraded |
| **Summative** | Measure achievement at the end of a learning period | Final exams, major assignments, capstone projects | Yes |
| **Diagnostic** | Identify prior knowledge or gaps before instruction begins | Pre-course survey, diagnostic quiz | No |

**Key principle:** Learners need more formative assessment than most courses provide. Each piece of formative feedback is an opportunity to correct misconceptions before they're reinforced.

## Assessment Types

### Traditional Assessments
- **Selected response:** Multiple choice, true/false, matching — efficient to grade; best for recall and recognition; easy to auto-grade
- **Short answer / constructed response:** Requires synthesis; harder to auto-grade but more revealing
- **Essay / long-form writing:** Excellent for higher-order thinking; resource-intensive to grade

### Authentic Assessments
Authentic assessments ask learners to apply knowledge in real-world or simulated contexts. They tend to be more engaging, more meaningful, and harder to outsource.

| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Case study analysis** | Apply concepts to a realistic scenario | Analyse a business failure using course frameworks |
| **Portfolio** | Curated collection of work over time | Design portfolio with reflective commentary |
| **Performance task** | Demonstrate a skill | Conduct a mock client interview |
| **Project-based** | Produce a real deliverable | Design a training program for a real organisation |
| **Presentation / pitch** | Communicate learning to an audience | Present a policy proposal to peers |
| **Simulation** | Practice in a safe, realistic environment | Clinical simulation; business decision game |
| **Reflection** | Meta-cognitive examination of learning | Weekly learning journals; end-of-course reflection |
| **Peer review** | Evaluate and give feedback on others' work | Structured peer critique using a rubric |

## Aligning Assessments to Bloom's Taxonomy

Bloom's Taxonomy describes levels of cognitive complexity. Assessment tasks should be matched to the level of thinking the outcome requires.

| Level | Verbs | Suggested Assessment Types |
|---|---|---|
| **Remember** | Define, list, recall, identify | Quiz, matching, flashcard |
| **Understand** | Explain, summarise, classify, describe | Short answer, concept map, discussion |
| **Apply** | Use, demonstrate, solve, implement | Problem set, case study, worked example |
| **Analyse** | Compare, differentiate, examine, break down | Case analysis, critique, comparison essay |
| **Evaluate** | Justify, assess, argue, critique | Debate, policy paper, peer review |
| **Create** | Design, construct, develop, produce | Project, portfolio, prototype, performance |

> 🔑 **A common design error:** Writing outcomes at the Apply or Analyse level, then assessing at Remember. The assessment reveals what you actually value.

## Constructive Alignment

Constructive alignment (Biggs & Tang) means that learning outcomes, teaching/learning activities, and assessment tasks all point toward the same goal.

**Alignment check questions:**
- Does this assessment task actually require learners to demonstrate the stated outcome?
- Do the learning activities in the course prepare learners to succeed on this assessment?
- Would a learner who achieved the outcome pass this assessment? Would a learner who didn't fail it?

**Alignment table format:**

| Learning Outcome | Assessment Task | Learning Activity |
|---|---|---|
| Analyse a case using [framework] | Written case analysis (30%) | Guided case practice in Week 4; class discussion |
| Demonstrate [skill] in context | Recorded performance task | Scaffolded practice activities in Weeks 2–5 |

## Academic Integrity by Design

Academic integrity issues are often symptoms of poor assessment design. The following strategies reduce the conditions that make dishonesty tempting or easy.

### Assessment Design Strategies
- **Authentic tasks** are harder to outsource or fabricate — the more specific to the learner's context, the better
- **Scaffolded assignments** with checkpoints (proposal → draft → peer review → final) make last-minute fabrication harder
- **Personalised prompts** — "Use your own workplace context" or "reflect on a moment from your own experience"
- **Process documentation** — ask learners to show their thinking, not just their conclusions
- **Varied assessment formats** across the course — rely on more than one high-stakes assignment
- **Oral components** — a brief follow-up conversation or viva can confirm authorship

### Using AI Tools Thoughtfully
AI tools have changed the academic integrity landscape. Consider:
- **AI-permitted tasks:** Design assessments that leverage AI well (e.g., use AI to generate a draft, then critically evaluate it)
- **AI-limited tasks:** Design tasks where AI cannot replicate the required evidence (personal reflection, live performance, contextual application)
- **Explicit policies:** Work with SMEs to develop clear, course-specific AI use policies communicated to learners upfront

## Feedback Design

Assessment without feedback is a missed learning opportunity.

**Principles of effective feedback:**
- **Timely:** Given while learners can still act on it
- **Specific:** Tied to the work, not generic praise or criticism
- **Forward-looking:** Tells learners what to do next, not just what was wrong
- **Calibrated:** Matches the level of effort the task requires

**Feedback mechanisms:**
| Mechanism | Best for |
|---|---|
| Rubric + written comments | Major assignments |
| Automated quiz feedback | Formative quizzes; factual recall |
| Peer feedback (structured) | Drafts; process skills |
| Audio/video feedback | Complex tasks where nuance matters |
| Model answers / exemplars | After summative assessment to support transfer |

## Rubric Design

A good rubric:
- Reflects the learning outcome it's assessing (not just effort or presentation)
- Uses **descriptive** language at each level (not just "good," "adequate," "poor")
- Is shared with learners **before** the assessment task
- Is usable by someone other than the instructor who designed it

**Rubric types:**
- **Holistic:** Single overall judgment — fast to apply; less actionable feedback
- **Analytic:** Separate criteria rated independently — slower; more specific feedback
- **Single-point:** One column describing proficient performance; annotated for above/below — simple; encourages dialogue

## Assessment Report Checklist

Use this when completing Stage 7 (Assessment Report) of the workflow:

- [ ] All learning outcomes have at least one associated assessment task
- [ ] Assessment types are varied (not all essays or all quizzes)
- [ ] Formative and summative assessments are both present
- [ ] Bloom's levels of outcomes and assessments are aligned
- [ ] Academic integrity strategies are embedded
- [ ] Rubrics or marking criteria are designed or planned
- [ ] Feedback mechanisms are defined for each assessment
- [ ] Weighting reflects the relative importance of each outcome
- [ ] SME has reviewed and approved the assessment strategy
