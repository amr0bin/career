# 10 · Stakeholder and SME Collaboration

## The Collaborative Foundation of Learning Design

No learning design project succeeds without strong relationships. The LXD's effectiveness is as much relational as it is technical — your ability to build trust, navigate disagreement, manage expectations, and bring out the best in SMEs directly determines the quality of the work.

This file covers how to build and sustain productive working relationships across the project lifecycle.

---

## Understanding Your Stakeholders

### Types of Stakeholders in Learning Design Projects

| Stakeholder | Role | What They Care About |
|---|---|---|
| **Subject Matter Expert (SME) / Faculty** | Primary content collaborator; owns the learning outcomes | Content accuracy; their time; student success; sometimes: control |
| **Department Head / Program Director** | Approves scope; may set non-negotiable requirements | Program outcomes; accreditation; budget; timelines |
| **Students / Learners** | The end users of everything you design | Clarity, relevance, fairness, workload, flexibility |
| **LMS / IT Administrator** | Manages the technical infrastructure | Stability, integration, security, support load |
| **Accessibility / Equity Office** | Advises on accommodation and inclusive design | WCAG compliance; equitable access |
| **Learning Design Unit Manager** | Your manager; manages resourcing and institutional relationships | Quality, capacity, team workload, institutional reputation |
| **Instructional Technologists / Media Producers** | Specialist contributors to your project | Clear briefs; realistic timelines; good feedback |

### Stakeholder Mapping

For complex projects, map your stakeholders across two axes:
- **Influence** (how much power they have over the project)
- **Interest** (how invested they are in the outcome)

| | High Interest | Low Interest |
|---|---|---|
| **High Influence** | Manage closely — keep informed, involve in key decisions | Keep satisfied — update on milestones; don't overload |
| **Low Influence** | Keep informed — they care and can provide useful input | Monitor — minimal effort needed |

## SME Onboarding

First impressions set the tone for the entire collaboration. The SME onboarding session (Stage 5 of the workflow) is your opportunity to establish norms, build trust, and get the collaboration off to a strong start.

### SME Onboarding Session Agenda (60–90 min)

1. **Introduction and relationship-building** (10 min)
   - Who you are and what your role is
   - Brief overview of the LXD team and how you work
   
2. **Overview of the design process** (15 min)
   - Walk through the workflow stages that apply to this project
   - Show examples of previous work (with permission) so they have a concrete picture
   - Emphasise: this is a collaborative process, not something you do *to* them

3. **Clarifying roles and responsibilities** (15 min)
   - What you will do (design, coordinate, build, facilitate)
   - What they will do (content, accuracy review, feedback, final approval)
   - What decisions they own vs. what you will recommend
   
4. **Communication norms** (10 min)
   - Preferred channel (email, Teams, etc.)
   - Meeting cadence
   - Turnaround expectations for review tasks
   - How to surface concerns or disagreements
   
5. **Timeline overview** (10 min)
   - Key milestones and dates
   - Their time commitment per week/sprint
   - Hard deadlines and flexibility zones

6. **Questions and next steps** (10 min)
   - What questions do they have?
   - Confirm first concrete next step and who owns it

## Managing the SME Relationship

### The Three Things SMEs Most Need From You
1. **Clarity** — to know what is expected of them and when
2. **Trust** — to feel their expertise is respected and valued
3. **Progress** — to see the project moving forward and not stalling

### Common SME Collaboration Challenges

**Challenge: The SME wants to add more and more content**
> *"We should really include a section on X... and also Y... and maybe Z..."*

Response strategy:
- Acknowledge the value of the suggestion
- Redirect to the learning outcomes: "That's interesting — let's look at whether it's essential for learners to achieve outcome 3, or whether it might be better suited to a follow-up course or supplementary resource"
- Use the MoSCoW framework (see `02_project_intake_and_scoping.md`) to prioritise together

**Challenge: The SME is unavailable or misses review deadlines**
Response strategy:
- Set up review cycles with clear deadlines and what happens if they're missed (the sprint shifts, not the launch date)
- Use async review tools (Google Docs comments, Loom video, Articulate Review) to lower the barrier to reviewing
- Escalate to the project plan: "We agreed to a review turnaround of 5 business days — can we check in on what's creating the delay?"

**Challenge: The SME wants to make the course they would want, not the course learners need**
Response strategy:
- Return to the learner personas and needs assessment data
- Ask: "How would a first-year student encounter this? What would they need to know before this makes sense to them?"
- Bring in student voice where possible (previous feedback, learner advisory input)

**Challenge: The SME doesn't trust the design process or wants to skip steps**
Response strategy:
- Be transparent about why each step matters and what problems it prevents
- Share examples of what happens when steps are skipped (project that went off the rails; assessment misalignment discovered at launch)
- Find ways to make the process feel light and fast — not bureaucratic
- Pick your battles: some steps can be abbreviated; none should be skipped entirely

**Challenge: The SME disagrees with your instructional recommendation**
Response strategy:
- Acknowledge their perspective genuinely — they know their learners and discipline
- Share the pedagogical rationale and relevant evidence clearly, without being condescending
- Offer a third option that bridges the gap
- If it's a disagreement of professional opinion (not a values conflict), defer to the SME with a documented note that you flagged the concern

## Giving and Receiving Feedback

### Giving Feedback to SMEs
- Be specific and focused on the work, not the person
- Frame as questions where possible: "I'm wondering if learners might find this sequence confusing — what do you think about trying X?"
- Separate technical accuracy concerns (which require correction) from design judgment (where you can negotiate)
- Give feedback in layers: big picture before details (don't redline sentence-level edits on a draft that has structural problems)

### Receiving Feedback from SMEs
- Separate feedback that changes the brief (scope change) from feedback that improves the work (welcome)
- Ask for specific feedback rather than open-ended "What do you think?" — use structured review prompts
- Document all feedback and decisions in a shared log
- Confirm your interpretation before acting on ambiguous feedback

### Structured Review Prompts
Give SMEs clear guidance on what kind of feedback you need at each review:

| Review Type | What You're Asking |
|---|---|
| **Accuracy review** | "Please flag anything that is factually incorrect or incomplete" |
| **Tone/voice review** | "Does this sound like the course you'd be proud to teach? Is anything off-putting or unclear?" |
| **Learner lens review** | "Put yourself in the shoes of a first-year student — what would be confusing or missing?" |
| **Final sign-off** | "This is ready to launch in your view — any last concerns?" |

## Managing Scope Creep

Scope creep is the gradual expansion of project requirements beyond what was originally agreed. It is one of the most common and costly challenges in learning design projects.

**Prevention:**
- Write a clear scope statement at intake (see `02_project_intake_and_scoping.md`)
- Use the scope statement as a reference point throughout the project
- When new requests come in, explicitly evaluate them against scope: "That's a great idea — it's outside our current scope, but let's add it to a Phase 2 backlog"
- Track all scope changes in a change log with the date, what was requested, and what was decided

**When scope creep happens anyway:**
- Name it clearly and without blame: "This is a significant addition to what we scoped — let's talk about what we adjust to accommodate it (timeline, another element we cut, additional resources)"
- Don't absorb scope changes silently — they become resentment and burnout
- Escalate to your manager if the SME insists on scope changes that you cannot accommodate

## Communication Best Practices

- **Confirm everything in writing.** Verbal agreements are forgotten or remembered differently. Follow up meetings with a brief email summary: "Just to confirm what we agreed today..."
- **Use a shared communication channel.** One place for project files, one place for messages — not scattered across email, Teams, text, and verbal hallway conversations.
- **Signal status proactively.** Don't wait for SMEs to chase you — send brief updates even when nothing dramatic has changed.
- **Name problems early.** A problem shared at Week 2 is manageable. The same problem shared at Week 8 is a crisis.
- **Acknowledge emotions, then redirect to the work.** If an SME is frustrated or anxious, name it and validate it briefly before moving on to the practical solution.
