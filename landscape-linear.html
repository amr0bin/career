Learning Design Specialization

A learning design practice, mapped

Much of what people need in order to act — on their health, their rights, their work — is not missing but unusable: built without them, for conditions that are not theirs. Learning design is where those choices are made. This practice develops knowledge through interaction with the people it concerns, and treats access as a condition of the work rather than a correction to it. This page sets out part of that practice: a trunk of shared craft, and five branches of expertise.

Section 1

How this map is organized

A single practice that designs how people and organizations learn, expressed as a trunk of shared craft and five connected branches of expertise — each with its own node and its own page. The trunk is what everything else grows from: the diagnostic question asked before anything is built, the writing, the visual language, the review against shared standards, the ethical and legal obligations the work carries, and the practices that keep it checkable and current. Every contribution below grows on one branch and links to the others it draws on, because the branches are not separate skills but one body of work seen from different perspectives.

The interactive version arranges the same material two ways: by branch, which shows where each contribution grows, and by affinity, which repositions contributions by how alike they are, so relationships across branches become visible.

A small number of contributions grow on two branches at once. Each appears once here, under its home branch, and states its second membership. A branch page may therefore list more resources than this page groups under that branch.

The shared craft of learning design, which every branch grows from and none of them owns. Minimum Viable Content

Deciding what to cut, before anything is built

Planned

Plain Language and Writing for Learning

Reading load as a design decision

Planned

How hard something is to read is usually the biggest barrier to understanding it, and it has nothing to do with the subject itself. It is also the barrier designers most often leave to chance. How Learning Works, and Who Can Act on It

Told plainly to learners, and built into the design

Planned

Most learners have never been told how consolidation works, why re-reading feels productive and isn’t, or that difficulty is often the signal rather than the failure. This makes that knowledge available in plain language as something offered rather than assigned, and builds the regulating into the design — spacing in the schedule, retrieval in the activity, requirements stated plainly — because every strategy in this literature presupposes conditions that unequal circumstances do not distribute equally.

Grounding. Learning How to Learn (Oakley & Sejnowski); desirable difficulties (Bjork); retrieval practice (Roediger & Karpicke); co-regulated and socially shared regulation (Hadwin & Järvelä); scarcity and bandwidth (Mullainathan & Shafir)

Supporting Indigenous-Led Learning Design

A settler designer’s role, named at its actual size

Planned

Design craft placed in service of Indigenous-led education and directed by Indigenous knowledge holders — never a claim to Indigenous knowledge, which is held in relationship and community and does not transfer to a portfolio. The scholarship belongs to Ramsden, Kirkness and Barnhardt, Archibald, Battiste, Ermine, and Tuhiwai Smith; what is offered here is process, plain language, accessibility, and production discipline in support of work whose direction is not the designer’s to set.

Cultural safety is determined by the person receiving the care or the teaching, never asserted by the person providing it — Ramsden’s principle, from the origin of the concept in Māori nursing scholarship. A designer can work toward an environment being safer, and cannot certify it safe on someone else’s behalf. This contribution describes contribution, not authority: it says what the design craft supplied and under whose direction.

Grounding. Cultural safety (Ramsden); the Four R’s — respect, relevance, reciprocity, responsibility (Kirkness & Barnhardt); Indigenous Storywork (Archibald); ethical space of engagement (Ermine); Decolonization Is Not a Metaphor (Tuck & Yang). Instruments: TRC Calls to Action 23 and 24; UNDRIP; BC’s Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act; the First Nations principles of OCAP®; FNHA cultural safety and humility commitments.
